## Abstract

1 Proteins are the main drivers of biochemical processes and play a pivotal role in 2 almost all cellular functions. Through post-translational modifications (PTMs), 3 residues within a protein can be chemically modified to fine-tune the protein's 4 function in the cellular context. Despite the importance of PTMs, the plethora 5 of deep learning-based de novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) models, which, in 6 contrast to database searching approaches, predict peptide sequence solely from 7 tandem mass spectra without any reference organism database, can only predict 8 peptide sequences with a limited set of PTMs. This is because they rely on fixed 9 vocabularies that map residue tokens to non-generalizable learned embeddings. To 10 overcome this limitation, we propose a novel approach that leverages the fact that 11 amino acids and their derivatives are characterized by their mass, a generalizable 12 feature that enables zero-shot learning. Specifically, we reformulate DNPS as a 13 mass prediction problem instead of a multiclass classification problem, where the 14 model predicts the mass of the next residue instead of its token representation. To 15 facilitate generalization to unseen PTMs, we leverage an adversarial multi-task 16 learning scheme by supplementing the training data of experimental spectra with 17 simulated spectra that mimic spectra containing unseen residues. We show that our 18 approach allows the prediction of previously unseen PTMs, providing a promising 19 proof of concept for mass-based representations as a path towards true open-search 20 DNPS.

## 21 **1 Introduction**

22 Proteins are essential to nearly all biological processes, functioning as catalysts, structural components, 23 signaling molecules, transporters, and immune effectors [1, 2]. Their functional diversity is further 24 enhanced by post-translational modifications (PTMs)—chemical changes to amino acid side chains 25 that influence protein structure and activity and are often linked to disease [3]. Proteomics, the study of 26 proteins, depends on accurate protein identification for downstream analyses such as quantification and 27 interaction mapping [4]. Bottom-up proteomics via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry 28 (LC-MS/MS) is the most common method for high-throughput protein identification [4, 5]. Here, 29 proteins are enzymatically digested into peptides, which are analyzed in a first mass spectrometry 30 scan to determine their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios. Selected precursor ions are then fragmented, and 31 the resulting fragment ions are measured in a second scan called a tandem mass spectrum [4]. Peptide 32 sequences can, in principle, be inferred from m/z differences between consecutive peaks [6, 7], but 33 this remains challenging due to missing or noisy peaks, co-isolated contaminants, and uncertainty 34 about ion series assignment. 35 Peptide sequences are typically identified using database search methods, which compare experimen36 tal spectra to theoretical spectra derived from a reference protein database [8]. Including PTMs further 37 expands the space of candidate peptides, making exhaustive searches computationally challenging. 38 De novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) bypasses the need for a reference database by inferring peptide 39 sequences directly from tandem mass spectra. This makes it, in contrast to database search methods, 40 well-suited for identifying novel peptides, rare or unknown PTMs, and proteins from unsequenced 41 organisms. Several de novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) tools have been introduced in recent years, 42 demonstrating promising performance across benchmark datasets [9–25]. However, accurately se43 quencing post-translationally modified (PTM) peptides remains a significant challenge. Despite 44 the existence of over 400 known PTM types [26, 27], Casanovo—the first transformer-based de 45 novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) model—can identify only seven [11]. Improving PTM prediction 46 has attracted considerable research interest, with recent advances such as AdaNovo [9]. Another 47 approach is to expand model coverage by enlarging the PTM vocabulary; for example, π-PrimeNovo 48 is fine-tuned to recognize 21 PTMs [28]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no current DNPS 49 model can detect PTMs not seen during training in a zero-shot manner, restricting their ability to 50 discover novel or rare PTMs.

51 In this work, we propose a transformer-based mass prediction approach to peptide sequencing, 52 enabling zero-shot inference for unseen residues, including novel PTMs. To improve generalization, 53 we explore multi-task learning (MTL) with a training strategy combining experimental and simulated 54 spectra. In the experimental spectra, the model encounters high spectral complexity with a limited 55 set of PTMs, while in the simulated spectra, it learns to generalize to unseen PTMs with arbitrary 56 peak differences. We introduce a generative adversarial network (GAN)-inspired MTL model, which 57 demonstrates strong performance on simulated data and limited but encouraging generalization 58 to unseen residues on experimental spectra. While not solving zero-shot residue inference on 59 experimental data, we provide a framework for future research and highlight key challenges.

## 60 **2 Background And Related Work**

61 In bottom-up proteomics, proteins are extracted from a biological sample and enzymatically di62 gested—typically with trypsin—into smaller peptides [4]. These peptides are separated by liquid 63 chromatography (LC) and introduced into a tandem mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS), typically 64 operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. In the first MS stage (MS1), peptide ions are 65 detected to determine their precursor masses and intensities. The most intense precursors are selected 66 for fragmentation in the second MS stage (MS2), generating spectra composed of fragment ion peaks 67 (tandem mass spectra).[5] The mass differences between these peaks correspond to the masses of 68 individual (modified) amino acids, enabling peptide sequence inference. [6, 7] Peptides are then 69 identified either by matching spectra to theoretical peptides from a reference proteome with database 70 search methods [29] or by interpreting spectra directly with de novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) 71 methods. Identified peptides are subsequently used to infer protein presence and abundance in the 72 original sample. [30]
73 In the early days of DNPS-based proteomic studies, spectra were manually annotated by experts—a 74 process that was both time-consuming and expensive [30]. As LC-MS/MS throughput increased, 75 early computational tools such as PEAKS [31], NovoHMM [32], and PepNovo [33] were developed 76 to automate de novo peptide sequencing. However, despite their theoretical potential, these methods 77 were often limited in accuracy and struggled with noisy or complex spectra, making database search 78 methods the preferred choice for many applications. 79 The field has gained renewed momentum with the rise of deep learning. DeepNovo [34] first 80 combined CNNs and LSTMs to model the peptide sequencing process directly. Following this, 81 PointNovo [25] employed a pointer network approach to enhance the decoding of peptide sequences. 82 Transformer-based architectures then marked a significant leap in performance, with Casanovo 83 [11] introducing a streamlined, attention-based model that boosted both accuracy and inference 84 speed. Several other transformer-based tools have emerged since [9, 10, 12, 17, 18, 24, 35], notably 85 AdaNovo, which incorporated adaptive learning strategies to improve performance on peptides 86 containing post-translational modifications (PTMs). Additionally, GraphNovo [16] introduced a 87 graph-based approach to capture the relationships between peptide fragments, further improving 88 sequencing accuracy and handling complex modification patterns. ContraNovo [12] enhanced the 89 transformer decoder of Casanovo by incorporating mass information directly into the model, allowing 90 it to better utilize the mass differences between peptide fragments and improve the accuracy of 91 peptide sequence prediction, particularly for spectra with ambiguous or overlapping peaks. Recently, 92 π-PrimeNovo was proposed, a model fine-tuned on 21 PTMs, to expand PTM coverage [24]. 93 These advancements have greatly enhanced the accuracy, generalization, and speed of DNPS tools, 94 making them more viable for applications with incomplete reference databases. However, DNPS 95 models capable of generalizing beyond the amino acids and PTMs present in the training data are 96 still lacking, limiting their ability to accurately discover novel peptides or uncharacterized PTMs.

## 97 **3 Methods** 98 **3.1 Datasets** 99 **3.1.1 Experimental Datasets**

100 We use the MassIVE Knowledge Base spectral library v1 (MassIVE-KB v1), originally introduced by 101 Casanovo for DNPS, to train and evaluate our method [11, 36]. This extensive dataset consists of high102 resolution HCD mass spectrometry data from diverse experimental conditions, totaling 30,504,897 103 peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) with extremely stringent false discovery rate (FDR) control [11]. 104 To facilitate direct comparison with Casanovo [11], we employ the same train, validation, and test 105 splits used in their study. The dataset includes peptide sequences composed of the 20 canonical amino 106 acids, along with eight post-translationally modified amino acids.

## 107 **3.1.2 Simulated Datasets**

108 To establish a model with zero-short learning capacity, we simulated synthetic spectra with a wide 109 variety of possible masses. We reasoned that the simulated masses did not have to match the masses of 110 existing PTM-amino acids. This incentivized models to capture the mechanisms of mass spectrometry 111 rather than memorizing predefined sets of masses. 112 We first generated peptide sequences as lists of residue masses, each sampled uniformly from the 113 range [60, 300] Da. Peptide lengths were drawn from a uniform distribution between 5 and 20 114 residues, and precursor charges z were sampled from a discrete distribution: z = 1: (0.5), z = 2: 115 (0.25), z = 3: (0.125), and z = 4: (0.125). 116 We implemented two strategies for generating the corresponding tandem mass spectra. In the first, 117 simplified approach, we generated an ideal spectrum consisting of one peak per residue, corresponding 118 directly to its mass over charge ratio (m/z). The second, more realistic strategy simulates peptide 119 fragmentation by generating b- and y-ions. More specifically, let z be the precursor charge and MPep = (m1, m2*, . . . , m*l) the vector of masses for a given peptide of length l, where mi 120 is the mass of the i-th residue. ## 130 **3.2 Model Architecture** 131 **3.2.1 Transformer-Based Mass Prediction**

132 Our model builds upon the transformer architecture introduced in Casanovo [11], but reformulates 133 the peptide sequencing task from next-token classification over (modified) amino acid tokens to a 134 continuous mass prediction task. For this, we introduce the mass regression decoder, which outputs a 135 scalar value representing the mass of each peptide residue in Dalton. We implement this by extending 136 Casanovo's peptide decoder with a four-layer feed-forward network with PReLU activation and a 137 single output dimension.

138 For each prediction step, the decoder is conditioned not only on the encoded spectrum and precursor 139 information (mass and charge) but also on the masses of previously predicted residues and the 140 remaining precursor mass, similar to [12]. This setup enables autoregressive decoding of arbitrary 141 residue masses consistent with the spectrum. 142 To map predicted masses back to residue tokens, we use an extendable mass lookup table, similar to 143 the approach in Contranovo [12]. Specifically, we map each predicted mass to the PTM–amino acid 144 combination that most closely matches its value. Token probabilities are computed via a softmax 145 over the negative absolute differences between the predicted mass and each entry in the table. By 146 extending this set post-training (e.g., by incorporating additional PTMs), the model can predict tokens 147 beyond those encountered during training.

## 148 **3.2.2 Gan-Inspired Latent Alignment**

149 To align representations of experimental and simulated spectra in a shared latent space, we adopt 150 a generative adversarial network (GAN)-inspired framework. The generator in this setup is the 151 transformer encoder, which encodes input spectra into latent vectors. These representations are 152 mean-pooled and passed to a discriminator—a three-layer feed-forward neural network (FNN) with 153 Leaky ReLU activations—adapted from the discriminator used by Wu et al. [37]. The discriminator 154 is trained to distinguish between embeddings of experimental and simulated spectra, while the DNPS
155 model is adversarially regularized by the discriminator's loss, thereby encouraging the encoder to 156 learn modality-invariant representations.

## 157 **3.3 Mulit-Task Training Strategy**

158 We train the model on a mixture of simulated and experimental spectra, sampling balanced batches 159 from both sources. The mass prediction model is optimized using a mean squared error (MSE) loss 160 LReg between predicted and ground truth residue masses. To train the adversarial extension of the 161 multi-task learning (MTL) model, we incorporate an additional regularization term based on the 162 discriminator's binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss LD. The MTL model is trained on a composite 163 loss LAdv, defined as a linear combination of the mass regression loss LReg (weight 1) and the 164 discriminator loss LD (weight -50).

165 The MTL model weights are initialized with weights from a model pre-trained solely on simulated 166 spectra from our simplified simulation approach for 80,000 steps. We train for 600,000 steps with a 167 batch size of 64 (approximately 1.5 epochs) on a single A40 GPU with 8 CPU cores and 60 GB of CPU RAM for approximately 2 days. The learning rate is set to 4 × 10−4 168 for the mass prediction DNPS model and 1 × 10−6 169 for the discriminator. The validation set was evaluated every 50,000 170 training steps, and the final model corresponds to the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss.

## 171 **4 Experiments** 182 **4.1 Evaluation Metrics** 198 **4.2 Main Results** 199 **4.2.1 Mass Regression Decoder Confidently Predicts Masses Seen During Training**

172 To enable de novo peptide sequencing (DNPS) models to generalize beyond the set of amino acids 173 and post-translational modifications (PTMs) observed during training, we reformulated the task 174 as a continuous mass prediction problem. Instead of selecting residues from a fixed vocabulary, 175 our model predicts a scalar mass for each peptide position, allowing for the potential inference of 176 arbitrary or novel modifications. These predicted masses are then mapped to residue identities via 177 a flexible, extendable lookup table. To encourage generalization, we adopt a multi-task training 178 strategy that combines complex experimental spectra, limited to a known set of PTMs, with simulated 179 spectra engineered to include a broad range of synthetic modifications. Additionally, we introduce a 180 GAN-inspired architecture, which aims to align latent representations of real and simulated spectra 181 and improve the model's ability to bridge between the two domains. 183 To assess the model's ability to generalize to modified amino acids not seen during training, we 184 excluded all spectra containing methionine oxidation and cysteine carbamidomethylation from the 185 training and validation sets—effectively treating these modifications as unseen during evaluation. 186 We evaluated performance at two levels: mass accuracy and residue identification. Mass accuracy 187 was measured as the absolute difference between the predicted and ground truth masses for each 188 residue. Residue identification was assessed by mapping each predicted mass to the nearest entry 189 in a lookup table containing all amino acid and PTM combinations present in the MassIVE-KB v1 190 dataset. The predicted residue was then compared to the ground truth to compute recall at the amino 191 acid level. 192 For evaluation, we ran the model in inference mode using teacher forcing. In this setup, the model 193 is supplied with the ground truth masses of all previously decoded residues at each prediction step. 194 This prevents error accumulation from incorrect predictions and isolates the model's ability to predict 195 each residue mass independently. Additionally, by enforcing the correct number of decoding steps, 196 teacher forcing ensures that the predicted and ground truth residue sequences are aligned, facilitating 197 direct comparison of their respective masses. 200 Our results indicated that recasting peptide sequencing as a mass prediction task preserved the 201 model's ability to infer residue identities. The model reliably predicted the masses of unmodified 202 residues seen during training on the defined test set (Fig. 2A). Across all unmodified residues seen 203 during training, our model achieved a median absolute error of approximately 0.56 Da on the test 204 set (Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.89, P-value<0.05). Errors ranged from 1.10 Da for tryptophan 205 (W) to 0.39 Da for glycine (G). This precision allowed the model to distinguish residues effectively, 206 resulting in an overall amino acid-level recall of 62.37%. Because we assigned predicted masses 207 to the closest matching residue in a lookup table, the model performed better for amino acids that 208 were well-separated in mass. For example, arginine (R) was correctly recalled in approximately 209 79.91% of cases. In contrast, the model struggled with residues whose masses are close to others, 210 such as lysine (K), which had a recall of only 26.97%—despite having a lower median error (0.48 211 Da) than arginine (0.61 Da). While these recall values were somewhat lower than those reported by 212 classification-based transformer models such as AdaNovo [9], this was expected given the nature of 213 our approach. Predicting scalar masses imposes a stricter requirement for precision: small deviations 214 could lead to mismatches when mapping back to discrete residue tokens. In contrast, models like 215 Casanovo [11] benefited from the flexibility of learned embedding spaces, where similar residues 216 could be placed further apart to ease classification. Despite this inherent challenge, our results 217 demonstrated that the mass-regression decoder effectively learned accurate mass representations for 218 residues seen during training, providing a viable foundation for generalization to modified residues 219 beyond the training set. 220 While the model successfully predicted the masses of residues seen during training, capturing a gener221 alizable and interpretable biochemical feature, it struggled to generalize to truly novel modifications 222 (Fig. 2B). For example, predictions for cysteine carbamidomethylation showed no enrichment near 223 the correct mass, with the distribution shifted toward lower values and a recall of only 0.6%. A 224 contributing factor to the failed extrapolation might be that all cysteines in the data were modified, 225 meaning the model never encountered an unmodified cysteine during training. In contrast, predictions 226 for methionine oxidation were modestly enriched around its correct mass, achieving a recall of 227 14.18%. However, this perhaps reflected a memorization artifact: the mass of methionine oxidation 228 (∼147.04 Da) was only ∼0.03 Da less than phenylalanine (F), a residue included in training, indi229 cating the model might simply be reproducing familiar masses. The observed bias toward masses 230 seen during training was consistent with trends in generalized zero-shot learning [38]. Since the 231 training objective does not explicitly encourage extrapolation to unseen masses, the model was not 232 incentivized to learn a truly continuous mass space. Instead, it might implicitly treat the task as a 233 form of multi-class classification, where the "classes" were the residue masses encountered during 234 training. As a result, the model could memorize these masses and reproduce them at inference time, 235 without being penalized by the loss function for failing to predict novel ones.

## 236 **4.2.2 Mass Regression Model Can Solve Simulated Open-Dnps Problem**

237 To mitigate the model's bias toward training-set residue masses—an issue arising from the limited 238 diversity of modifications in experimental spectra—we explored the use of simulated spectra spanning 239 a broader amino acid–PTM search space. In these simulations, peptides were constructed by sampling 240 random residue masses to mimic modified amino acids, thereby preventing memorization. We 241 designed three simulation levels of increasing complexity and trained a separate model on each.

242 Across all settings, the model successfully learned to predict the correct masses (Fig. 3A, B), though 243 performance declined as simulation complexity increased (Pearson correlation coefficients of 1.0, 244 0.99 and 0.97 with two-sided P-values<0.05). Introducing a variable number of peaks led to higher 245 mass prediction errors compared to simulations with a fixed number of peaks (Fig. 3B), as expected 246 from the increased difficulty. Interestingly, the model remained robust when noise peaks and realistic 247 fragmentation processes were introduced. However, these settings caused the MSE loss to more than 248 double, largely due to occasional large prediction errors (108.96 vs. 263.98). This is likely because 249 the simulation emulated missing peaks (although never both of the complementary b- and y-ion), 250 causing the model to infer residue masses from unrelated fragments, leading to strong deviations and 251 quadratic penalties from the MSE loss. 252 Although the model performed well on simulated spectra, it failed to generalize to experimental 253 spectra and accurately predict real residue masses (Pearson correlation coefficients of -0.11, 0.19 and 254 0.04 with two-sided P-values<0.05). We reasoned that the domain shift between simulated and real 255 data was too substantial, leading models trained on simplified simulations to break down entirely 256 when applied to real spectra (Fig. 3C). These models produced nearly identical mass distributions 257 across all residues, indicating a lack of residue-specific signal. The model trained on the most realistic 258 simulation—incorporating both noise peaks and fragmentation mechanics—showed some limited 259 signs of generalization to experimental spectra (Fig. 3C). ## 270 **4.2.3 Multi-Task Learning Improves Generalization**

271 We explored Multi-Task Learning (MTL) as a strategy to improve the model's ability to generalize, 272 particularly to unseen post-translational modifications. MTL has proven effective in various domain 273 adaptation contexts [39–41], and we adapted it by training the model on a balanced combination of real 274 and simulated spectra. The rationale was twofold: training on real experimental spectra encourages the 275 model to learn the inherent complexity and noise characteristics of true mass spectrometry data, while 276 training on simulated spectra—which include a diverse set of unrestricted residue masses—pushes 277 the model to generalize beyond the limited set of modifications seen in the real data. By combining 278 both sources, the model was encouraged to learn features that were robust across domains while 279 being flexible enough to infer arbitrary modifications. 280 The MTL model improved performance on simulated spectra (Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.98 281 with two-sided P-value<0.05, Fig. 4A) but sacrificed precision on seen residues in experimental spec282 tra compared to the model trained exclusively on experimental data (Pearson correlation coefficient: 283 0.83 with two-sided P-value<0.05, Fig. 4B, median absolute errors of 3.17 Da vs. 0.56 Da). On 284 the other hand, the MTL model outperformed the model trained solely on the simulated data with 285 a lower median absolute error of 2.95 Da compared to 4.25 Da. The imbalance between the MTL

## 307 **4.2.4 Adversarial Loss For Common Latent-Space For Spectrum Embedding**

308 Although the MTL model performed well on simulated data, it was still biased toward seen masses. 309 To investigate whether simulated data was effectively leveraged during training, we analyzed the 310 embeddings with a 2D PCA projection (Fig. 4D). The embeddings for experimental and simulated 311 spectra formed distinct clusters, suggesting that the transformer-based mass regression decoder could 312 easily differentiate between the two data types. This separation might have caused the model to treat 313 simulated spectra differently, reducing their effectiveness in helping the model generalize to realistic, 314 unseen masses. 315 To reduce the separation between simulated and experimental spectra, we incorporated adversarial 316 learning by adding a lightweight binary classifier to our MTL model that predicted whether a spectrum 317 was simulated or experimental based on its encoding. The classification loss was subtracted from the 318 MTL model's MSE loss to encourage a shared latent space for both types of spectra. Following the 319 addition of this adversarial loss, the embeddings of simulated and experimental spectra no longer 320 separated clearly in PCA space (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the discriminator's binary cross entropy loss 321 during training plateaued at around 0.68, meaning its predicted probabilities lay consistently around 322 0.5 (-log(0.5)≈0.69). However, the loss and PCA alone did not provide definitive evidence that the 323 model could not still distinguish between the two spectrum types. This was further supported by the 324 discriminator achieving an area under the ROC curve of around 0.78. 325 Unfortunately, the addition of the adversarial loss term did not lead to a noticeable improvement 326 in the MTL model's ability to generalize to unseen residues. The distributions of predicted masses 327 for unseen residues remained very similar between the MTL models with and without adversarial 328 loss (Pearson correlation coefficient: 0.82 with two-sided P-value<0.05, Fig. 4C). Additionally, both 329 models showed comparable recall rates for methionine oxidation (3.18% vs. 3.10%) and cystein 330 carbamidomethylation (14.41% vs. 15.11%), along with similar MSE losses.

## 331 **5 Conclusion And Future Work**

332 We present a novel framework for de novo peptide sequencing that reformulates the task as mass 333 regression rather than discrete classification. This shift enables the model to move beyond a fixed 334 vocabulary of amino acids and modifications, allowing it to predict residues not encountered during 335 training. Our approach combines transformer-based mass prediction with a multi-task learning setup 336 trained on both experimental and simulated spectra. To encourage domain-invariant representations, 337 we incorporate adversarial learning that aligns the spectrum encodings across data sources. Results 338 demonstrate promising generalization to previously unseen modifications, marking a step toward 339 more flexible and open-ended peptide sequencing. By enabling the discovery of novel or rare peptide 340 modifications, this work may support future advancements in biomedical research. 341 **Limitations.** This work serves as a proof of concept rather than a production-ready system. Although 342 we show that mass-based modeling can overcome vocabulary constraints, the current framework 343 has only been evaluated under teacher-forced decoding. Nonetheless, it provides a foundation 344 for developing fully open de novo sequencing models that support more reliable and practical 345 applications. 346 **Future directions.** Several avenues merit further investigation: exploring the tradeoff between scalar 347 mass prediction and vector-based encodings, improving robustness to noise in simulated spectra, 348 and developing loss functions that better prioritize precision. The role of adversarial learning also 349 warrants deeper analysis, particularly whether the model implicitly treats simulated data differently. 350 Incorporating high-fidelity simulated spectra (e.g., from models like Prosit [43, 44]) and more 351 advanced decoding strategies could further improve performance. Together, these advances could 352 enable DNPS models that generalize across biological contexts, capturing both known and novel 353 peptide modifications with greater reliability.

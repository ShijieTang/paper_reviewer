## Abstract 19 **1 Introduction**

20 In the field of industrial image anomaly detection, acquiring or predefining anomalous samples 21 is often impractical. Consequently, Unsupervised Anomaly Detection (UAD), which relies only 22 on normal samples for training, has been extensively studied [5, 7]. To tackle the challenges of 23 UAD task, a variety of methods are proposed, such as those based on memory banks [26, 2], 24 anomaly synthesis [19, 35], and image reconstruction [4, 1]. In recent years, UAD methods based on 25 Knowledge Distillation (KD) have gained increasing attention [6]. Compared to other techniques, 26 they show greater potential in pixel-level anomaly localization. The KD-based UAD methods 27 typically employ a teacher-student framwork, which allows the student network to imitate the feature 28 representations of the teacher on normal samples. Since the student is never exposed to anomalous 29 samples during training, its ability to generate teacher's anomaly features is limited. This discrepancy 30 in feature mimicking performance becomes a useful signal for anomaly identification. 31 In real-world scenarios, however, it is often inevitable that the collected data contain a small proportion 32 of anomalous samples. Relying entirely on manual data cleaning incurs high labor costs. This 33 motivates the need for Fully Unsupervised Anomaly Detection (FUAD), a more practical and 34 challenging setting where the training set may contain unlabeled anomalous samples. This work aims to explore this promising direction.

## 142 **3.2 Assumptions**

125 **Why does RD Work for FUAD?** Although Reverse Distillation (RD) is initially designed for training with only 126 normal samples, it demonstrates strong adaptability in Fully Unsupervised Anomaly Detection. We attribute this 127 to two key factors: 128 (1) **Probability Perspective** - Dominance of Normal Samples 129 In industrial scenarios, normal samples are much more common than anomalies, which results in low proportion 130 of anomalous images in the training set. Moreover, anomalies typically occupy only a small region within an 131 image. Consequently, the student network, driven by the dominance of normal samples, primarily learns to 132 represent normal features, while the sparsity of anomalies limits their impact on the optimization process. 133 (2) **Distribution Perspective** - Concentrated Normal vs. Diverse Anomalous 134 Normal samples exhibit compact and consistent feature patterns, while anomalous samples are diverse and 135 scattered. This makes it difficult for the student to generalize learned anomalous features. 136 **Challenges of Applying RD to FUAD.** In FUAD task, the training set naturally includes a certain proportion 137 of anomalous samples. If specific anomaly patterns appear repeatedly during training, the student can easily 138 learn to reconstruct the teacher features of these conmmon anomalies. This results in poor discrimination against 139 similar anomalies during testing and further intensifies over-generalization. Therefore, the key challenge in 140 applying RD to FUAD is *how to prevent the student from modeling common anomaly patterns during training,* 141 *to ensure that it generates anomaly-free features*.

143 To address over-generalization problem in FUAD, we propose two assumptions based on the diversity and 144 sparsity of anomalies, guiding the following design of our method Cross-Domain Distillation. 145 **Assumption 1 (Limited Representation of Rare Anomalies)** *When a particular anomaly type is sufficiently* 146 *rare in training data, the student fails to learn its corresponding teacher anomaly features, and instead tends to* 147 *produce features that closely resemble normal patterns.* 148 Due to the consistency of normal samples and the diversity of anomalies (i.e., anomalies exhibit multiple distinct 149 patterns), we assume the training set contains one normal type and M*train* anomaly types, expressed as: 150 where N denotes the set of normal samples, Am denotes the set of the m-th anomaly type, and:

## 195 **4.1 Domain-Specific Training**

196 **Confidence-Guided Domain Construction** Based on *Assumption 1*, reducing the anomaly probability 197 in the training set helps the student better learn normal patterns. A naive way to achieve this is to retain only 198 high-confidence normal samples or discard low-confidence anomalous ones. However, such strategies fail to 199 fully utilize the training data, as potentially useful normal regions are also discarded along with the anomalies. 200 To address this issue, we introduce a confidence-guided strategy on top of naive equal partitioning. Specifically, 201 we inject a portion of highly confident normal samples into each domain based on normality confidence scores, 202 which ensures that: (1) The anomaly ratio in each domain becomes lower than that in the original training set, 203 reducing the interference of anomalous samples on the modeling of normal patterns. (2) The normal distribution 204 in each domain remains more similar to the overall normal distribution, mitigating the negative impact of domain 205 partitioning on student normality modeling.

339 In this paper, we propose a novel Cross-Domain Distillation framework to address the FUAD task. To reduce 340 the impact of anomalies during training, we introduce two key strategies: Domain-Specific Training, which 341 constructs multiple low-anomaly domains and trains corresponding domain-specific students; and Cross-Domain 342 Knowledge Aggregation, which transfers pseudo-normal features in a cross-domain manner to guide a global 343 student. Compared with the original Reverse Distillation (RD) baseline, our approach significantly improves 344 robustness and accuracy under noisy training conditions. Compared with the original RD baseline, CDD is less 345 affected by anomaly interference under the FUAD setting, as supported by our experimental results. 346 **Discussion.** Although CDD is implemented based on the RD paradigm, the core design is conceptually 347 general and could be extended to other UAD paradigms. However, our experiments are restricted to RD-based 348 architectures. Future work will focus on adapting and validating CDD under other paradigms, such as feature 349 reconstruction, to further demonstrate its generality.

## Abstract

1 Existing blind face restoration (BFR) methods suffer from drastic performance drop 2 under severe degradations. A common strategy is to first remove degradations and 3 then restore the face by fully harnessing generative prior. However, this sequential 4 pipeline risks discarding subtle but crucial cues from already limited low-quality 5 (LQ) inputs. To address this, we ingeniously introduce a new learning paradigm: 6 simultaneous prior injection and degradation removal (SPIDER). Unlike existing 7 approaches, SPIDER injects semantic prior before degradation removal, thereby 8 preserving identity-relevant features and mitigating the impact of corrupted LQ 9 features. SPIDER consists of two key modules: (1) a prior injection module that 10 distills purified degradation-unaware semantic control tokens from vision-language 11 models, and (2) a degradation removal module equipped with an image-to-text 12 degradation mapper and a degradation remover that refines distorted features into 13 robust representations. Extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world 14 datasets, including challenging surveillance scenarios, demonstrate SPIDER's clear 15 superiority over state-of-the-art BFR methods. 17 Blind Face Restoration (BFR) is a challenging task that aims to recover high-quality (HQ) face 18 images from low-quality (LQ) ones that suffer from unknown and complex degradations such as low 19 resolution [3, 6], blur [48], noise [13, 28], and JPEG compression [5]. This is an inherently ill-posed 20 problem as the information loss caused by the degradations leads to an overwhelming number of 21 plausible HQ solutions consistent with the same LQ input. To mitigate the ill-posedness, recent 22 studies have explored various prior-based methods to produce high-fidelity outputs. 23 As illustrated in Figure 1, existing prior-based BFR methods fall into three main paradigms: 1) 24 Continuous generative prior (e.g., GFPGAN [36], which learns accurate latent codes via GAN inver25 sion to reconstruct HQ faces with high fidelity; 2) Discrete generative prior (e.g., Codeformer [52], 26 DAEFR [30]), which uses vector quantization to map degraded inputs into semantic tokens and 27 harness a fixed HQ codebook for high-quality restoration; and 3) Diffusion-based conditional genera28 tion (e.g., DiffBIR [18], FaithDiff [2]), which reframes the restoration into conditional generation 29 employing the powerful expressiveness of diffusion prior to achieve significant improvements in fine 30 detail, perceptual fidelity, and overall realism. Many SOTA methods [18, 2, 37, 41] belong to the 31 third paradigm and achieve promising results on mild to moderate degradations. However, under 32 severe or extreme degradation, whether synthetic or real-world, they often introduce artifacts or 33 even fail catastrophically in the results, as demonstrated in Figure 2. 41 To overcome this, we ingeniously propose Simultaneous Prior Injection and Degradation Removal 42 (SPIDER), a new learning paradigm to enhance face restoration (Figure 1 (d)). Rather than removing 43 degradation first, SPIDER injects semantic prior (i.e., combined with diffusion prior) before degrada44 tion removal. These semantic priors are subsequently injected into 51 the diffusion generation pipeline at multiple levels, providing robust and context-aware guidance. 52 The DRM comprises an image-to-text degradation mapper and a degradation remover, which together 53 project noisy visual representations into a purified textual embedding space aligned with the injected 54 prior. This design leverages the noise-resilience of the textual modality and performs degradation 55 filtering through semantic alignment, which is more robust to perturbations than direct visual-space 56 restoration. By jointly integrating semantic prior injection and degradation removal via our proposed 57 decoupled cross-attention (DCA) mechanism, SPIDER delivers state-of-the-art restoration results 58 under severe degradations in both synthetic and real-world scenarios. 59 SPIDER achieves state-of-the-art results on both existing synthetic and real-world benchmarks and 60 our newly introduced SCface dataset [8] of extreme surveillance face images. Beyond its superior 61 BFR performance, SPIDER pioneers a novel learning paradigm injecting prior before degradation 62 *removal* that can be extended to a wide range of restoration tasks beyond blind face restoration.

## 93 **2.2 Degradation Removal In Blind Image Restoration**

86 Although the above methods have demonstrated strong performance in restoring faces under moderate 87 degradations, they often struggle in real-world scenarios involving severe and complex degradations. 88 This results in visual artifacts, structural distortions, and semantic inconsistencies. A key challenge 89 lies in the model's difficulty in distinguishing intrinsic, reliable facial features from degradation90 induced noise. Consequently, synthesizing faces from corrupted or noisy representations can lead 91 to erroneous or unrealistic restoration outcomes. Therefore, effectively removing degradations is a 92 prerequisite for achieving faithful and high-quality restoration. 94 Recent blind image restoration methods increasingly focus on learning degradation processes to 95 enhance realism and adaptability. Due to the limitations of handcrafted degradation assumptions, 96 AND [35] introduces an adversarial degradation generator that synthesizes pseudo-degraded images, 97 thereby bridging the domain gap between synthetic and real-world degradations in supervised 98 restoration. DiffBIR [18] and FaithDiff [2] both adopt a two-stage design, where degradation 99 is first removed and then image quality is refined. TextualDegRemoval [17] leverages textural 100 modality representations to generate clean guidance images under natural degradations such as rain 101 and snow, using them as reference images to enhance blind image restoration. However, despite 102 differences in representation and guidance modality, these methods share a structurally decoupled 103 architecture: degradation removal is treated as a prerequisite, independent of the image generation 104 process. This decoupling hinders joint optimization and frequently leads to unstable outputs when 105 handling occlusion, motion blur, or structural corruption—especially in face restoration, where 106 identity consistency and semantic fidelity are particularly fragile.

## 107 **2.3 Vision-Language Models**

108 Vision-language models (VLMs) have advanced rapidly with CLIP [26] providing strong semantic 109 prior by aligning image and text embeddings. DA-CLIP [23] models image content and degradations 110 jointly, enabling multi-task restoration. SSP-IR [51] enhances geometric consistency by integrating 111 structural contour information into CLIP-based prior, while FUSION [21] improves cross-modal 112 understanding through deep feature fusion. Apart from general VLMs, researchers have also proposed 113 vision-language architectures specifically designed for face images. FCLIP [4] uses dual-branch 114 learning on FaceCaption-15M for better attribute alignment. Face-MLLM [29] employs a three-stage 115 strategy on a large-scale QA dataset to enhance fine-grained attribute reasoning and instruction 116 following. FaceInsight [14] integrates keypoint detection and attention mechanisms to ensure 117 structural and identity consistency. However, these methods remain unpublished or proprietary, 118 hindering the integration of face-oriented VLMs into BFR task.

## 119 **3 Proposed Method: Spider** 120 **3.1 Framework Overview**

121 As illustrated in Figure 3, our SPIDER restores HQ face images from their LQ counterparts by 122 simultaneously injecting cross-modal semantic prior and removing degradations. The training process 123 of SPIDER is divided into two stages. In Stage I (Figure 3(b)), we train a degradation removal 124 module (DRM) to remove degradations at the textual level, where the image content and degradation 125 information are loosely coupled, making it more effective to isolate and remove noise. In Stage 126 II (Figure 3(a)), we employ a large vision-language model (i.e., LLaVA) to generate detailed text 127 descriptions of HQ face images, enabling the extraction of fine-grained semantic prior. Meanwhile, 128 the pretrained DRM is used to "erase" feature corruption at multiple scales. These two branches (i.e., 129 prior injection and degradation removal) interact via a decoupled cross attention (DCA) mechanism 130 (Figure 3(c)) integrated into each block of both the UNet [27] and ControlNet [49]. This collaborative 131 design ensures robust guidance and effective noise suppression, ultimately leading to faithful HQ 132 face restoration.

## 133 **3.2 Semantic Prior Injection**

134 Many recent super-resolution models leverage prior knowledge provided by vision-language models, 135 such as FaithDiff [2], XPSR [25], and AuthFace [16]. The performance of LLaVA is highly dependent 136 on well-crafted prompts [20]. Considering the highly structured nature of human faces, we modify the 137 prompt design to focus the output on detailed facial component structures. Experiments in XPSR [25] 138 and SPIRE [24] demonstrate that LLaVA can implicitly infer both the type and severity of noise 139 artifacts from facial images, maintaining robust performance even under noisy conditions. In our 140 study, we extract structural descriptions of facial features from LLaVA and encode them using CLIP 141 text encoder to obtain embeddings and then further integrate these embeddings with DRM outputs 142 through a DCA module to guide the image restoration process.

## 143 **3.3 Degradation Removal Module (Drm)**

144 Effective degradation removal is essential for recovering high-fidelity HQ face images from LQ 145 inputs, as corrupted signals can mislead the blind face restoration (BFR) model. To address this, 146 we propose a novel cross-modal mapping module, DRM, which directly transforms degraded face 147 images into noise-suppressed textual representations. This cross-modal strategy is more effective than 148 conventional image-space denoising, as the textual embedding space exhibits a natural decoupling 149 between semantic content and degradation-induced noise. 150 As shown in Figure 3, the proposed DRM comprises two key components: (1) a Degradation Mapper 151 that projects CLIP image embeddings into implicit textual representations, preserving rich visual 152 semantics that are often lost in explicit textual descriptions; and (2) a Degradation Remover that 153 purifies these representations by filtering out degradation-specific artifacts. The resulting clean textual 154 features maintain high semantic fidelity to the original image content while eliminating noise patterns, 155 thereby providing reliable guidance for subsequent image generation.

## 195 **4 Experiments** 196 **4.1 Experimental Settings**

197 **Training Datasets.** We train all models on the FFHQ dataset [11], which contains 70,000 high198 quality face images resized to 512 × 512. We follow DiffBIR [18] to generate corresponding low199 quality (LQ) images. To obtain semantic guidance, we use LLaVA [19] to generate face description 200 prompts for each image. 201 **Testing Datasets.** We evaluate our method on one synthetic dataset and three real-world datasets. 202 The synthetic dataset, CelebA-Test [10], comprises 3,000 images sampled from CelebA-HQ, with LQ 203 versions generated using the same degradation pipeline as training. The real-world datasets include 204 LFW-Test [9] (1,711 images, mild degradation), WIDER-Test [42] (970 images, heavy degradation), 205 and SCface [8], which features extreme surveillance degradations such as aliasing, jagged artifacts, 206 low-light noise, and defocus blur. SCface contains 130 subjects captured by five surveillance cameras; 207 for evaluation, we use images taken from a distance of 2.6 meters. 208 **Compared Methods.** We compare our proposed SPIDER with seven state-of-the-art BFR methods 209 across three categories: (1) StyleGAN-prior methods: GFPGAN [36]; (2) Codebook-prior meth210 ods: CodeFormer [52] and DAEFR [30]; (3) Diffusion-based methods: DR2 [37], PGDiff [41], 211 DiffBIR [18] and FaithDiff [2]. All experiments are conducted using official code. 212 **Evaluation Metrics.** We adopt both reference-based and no-reference image quality metrics for 213 comprehensive evaluation. For synthesized datasets with ground truth, we use PSNR [38], SSIM [38], 214 LPIPS [50], FID [1], as well as no-reference perceptual metrics MANIQA [43] and CLIPIQA [33] 215 to better capture perceptual quality. For real-world datasets without ground truth, we evaluate 216 performance using MANIQA, CLIPIQA, and FID. 217 **Implementation Details** Both the Degradation Mapper and Remover are trained for one epoch on 218 the FFHQ dataset during Stage I, with a batch size of 4, using the Adam optimizer and a learning rate of 1 × 10−6 219 on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU. Our SPIDER model is built upon the pretrained 220 stable-diffusion-2.1 and trained for 15 epochs on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs, with a batch size of 192, using the Adam optimizer [12] and a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 221 .

## 291 **5 Conclusion**

292 We propose SPIDER, a novel paradigm for blind face restoration that performs simultaneous multi293 level prior injection and degradation removal. SPIDER adopts an interleaved architecture where prior 294 injection precedes degradation removal at each level, ensuring that semantic and diffusion priors 295 amplify both signal and noise in a way that helps the DRM better distinguish meaningful facial 296 features from heavy degradations. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets confirm its 297 superiority over state-of-the-art methods. Beyond its strong performance in BFR, SPIDER presents a 298 novel learning paradigm with broad applicability to various image restoration tasks.

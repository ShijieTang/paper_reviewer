# Accelerated Learning With Linear Temporal Logic Using Differentiable Simulation

18 **1 Introduction**
19 The growing demand for artificial intelligence (AI) systems to operate in a wide range of environments 20 underscores the need for systems that can learn through interaction with their environments, without 21 relying on human intervention. Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for 22 training controllers to perform effectively in uncertain settings with intricate, high-dimensional, and 23 nonlinear dynamics. Recent advances in RL have enabled attainment of high-performance controllers 24 in a variety of applications [1], such as robotic arm control [2], hand manipulation [3], legged 25 locomotion [4, 5], navigation in crowded spaces [6], and robot-assisted surgery [7]. Despite the 26 promising results in controlled environments, deploying learned controllers in real-world systems 27 –where malfunctioning can be costly or hazardous– requires not only high performance but also strict 28 compliance with formally specified safety and reliability requirements. Therefore, ensuring that 29 learned controllers meet these critical specifications is essential to fully realize the potential of AI 30 systems in real-world applications. Safety in learning is often modeled with constrained Markov 31 decision processes (MDPs) [8–12], where the accumulated cost must be within a budget. However, 32 additive cost functions may not reflect real-world safety, as assigning meaningful costs to harms is 33 challenging. Alternative approaches define safety by avoiding unsafe states or actions [13–27], which 34 is simpler than designing cost functions. However, this may result in overly conservative policies and 35 could not capture complex trajectory-level requirements. 36 Recently, researchers have explored specifying RL objectives using formal languages, which explic37 itly and unambiguously express trajectory-based task requirements, including safety and liveness 38 properties. Among these, linear temporal logic (LTL) has gained particular popularity [28–50] due 39 the automaton-based memory it offers, which ensures history-independence and makes it especially 40 suitable for long-horizon tasks unlike other languages such as signal temporal logic (STL). Specifying 41 desired properties in LTL inherently prevents mismatches between the intended behavior and the 42 behavior learned through reward maximization–one of the most well-known safety challenges in 43 AI [51]. ## 62 **2 Related Work**

63 **Safe RL.** One common perspective in Safe RL defines safety as the guarantee on the cumulative 64 costs over time within a specified safety budget, which is often modeled using constrained MDPs 65 and has been widely studied [8–12], relying on additive cost functions and budgets, which may not 66 adequately capture safety in many scenarios. In practice, it is often difficult to assign unambiguous 67 scalar costs reflecting trade-offs between different harmful situations [52]. Another approach defines 68 safety in terms of avoiding unsafe states and focuses on preventing or modifying unsafe actions via 69 shielding or barrier functions [13–27], which only require identification of unsafe states and actions 70 and often easier than designing cost functions [53]; however, they can lead to overly conservative 71 control policies [54]. Moreover, the requirements are often placed over trajectories, which could 72 be more complex than simply avoiding certain states [55]. Our approach avoids these issues by 73 employing LTL as the specifications language to obtain correct-by-construction RL objectives. 74 **RL with Temporal Logics.** There is growing increasing in using formal specification languages to 75 encode trajectory-dependent task objectives, especially those involving safety. LTL is widely used 76 due to its expressiveness and well-defined semantics over infinite traces. There has been increasing 77 interest in using formal specification languages to encode task objectives that are trajectory-dependent, 78 particularly those involving safety requirements. LTL has emerged as a widely adopted formalism 79 due to its expressiveness and well-defined semantics over infinite traces. Recent efforts [28–50] 80 derives rewards from LTL specifications for RL, typically by translating LTL into limit-deterministic 81 Büchi automata (LDBAs) and assigning rewards based on acceptance conditions. The memory 82 structure provided by LDBAs supports long-horizon tasks better than alternatives such as STL, which 83 require history-dependent rewards [56]. However, LTL rewards are often sparse and hinder learning. 84 While heuristic-based dense rewards [38] attempt to address this, they risk misguiding exploration 85 and compromising correctness. Our approach avoids these pitfalls by leveraging gradients from 86 differentiable simulators to accelerate learning without sacrificing correctness. 87 **RL with Differentiable Simulators.** Differentiable simulators enable gradient-based policy opti88 mization in RL by computing gradients of states and rewards with respect to actions, using analytic 89 methods [57–61] or auto-differentiation [62, 63]. While Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) is 90 commonly used [64–69], it suffers from vanishing or exploding gradients in long-horizon tasks as 91 it ignores the Markov property of states [70]. To address this, several differentiable RL algorithms 92 have been proposed [71, 72]. Short Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) [73] divides long trajectories into 93 shorter segments where BPTT is tractable and bootstraps the remaining trajectory using the value 94 function. Adaptive Horizon Actor-Critic (AHAC) [74] extends SHAC by dynamically adjusting the 95 segment lengths based on contact information from the simulator. Gradient-Informed PPO [75] incor96 porates gradient information into the PPO framework in an adaptive manner. Our approach builds a 97 differentiable, Markovian transition function for LTL-derived automata, making it compatible with 98 all differentiable RL methods. Unlike prior STL-based efforts [76, 77], which rely on non-Markovian 99 rewards and BPTT, our method supports efficient long-horizon learning with full differentiability.

## 380 **7 Conclusion**

381 In this work, we tackle the critical challenge of scalable RL for robotic systems under long-horizon, 382 formally specified tasks. By adopting LTL as our specification framework, we ensure objective 383 correctness and avoid the reward misspecification issues commonly encountered in conventional RL 384 approaches. To overcome the learning inefficiencies caused by sparse logical rewards, we propose a 385 novel method that leverages differentiable simulators, enabling gradient-based learning directly from 386 LTL objectives without compromising their expressiveness or correctness. Our approach introduces 387 soft labeling techniques that preserve the differentiability through the transitions of automata derived 388 from LTL formulas, resulting in end-to-end differentiable learning framework. Through a series of 389 simulated experiments, we demonstrate that our method substantially accelerates learning compared 390 to SOTA non-differentiable baselines, paving the way for more reliable and scalable deployment of 391 autonomous robotic systems in complex real-world environments.

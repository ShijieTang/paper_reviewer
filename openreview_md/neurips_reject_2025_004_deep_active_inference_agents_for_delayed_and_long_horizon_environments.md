## Abstract

1 With the recent success of *world-model* agents—which extend the core idea of 2 model-based reinforcement learning by learning a differentiable model for sample3 efficient control across diverse tasks—*active inference* (AIF) offers a complemen4 tary, neuroscience-grounded paradigm that unifies perception, learning, and action 5 within a single probabilistic framework powered by a generative model. Despite 6 this promise, practical AIF agents still rely on accurate *immediate* predictions 7 and exhaustive planning, a limitation that is exacerbated in *delayed* environments 8 requiring planning over *long horizons*—tens to hundreds of steps. Moreover, most 9 existing agents are evaluated on robotic or vision benchmarks which, while natural 10 for biological agents, fall short of real-world industrial complexity. We address 11 these limitations with a generative–policy architecture featuring (i) a *multi-step* 12 *latent transition* that lets the generative model predict an entire horizon in a single 13 look-ahead, (ii) an integrated policy network that enables the transition and receives 14 gradients of the expected free energy, (iii) an alternating optimization scheme that 15 updates model and policy from a replay buffer, and (iv) a single gradient step 16 that plans over long horizons, eliminating exhaustive planning from the control 17 loop. We evaluate our agent in an environment that mimics a realistic industrial 18 scenario with delayed and long-horizon settings. The empirical results confirm the 19 effectiveness of the proposed approach, demonstrating the coupled world-model 20 with the AIF formalism yields an end-to-end probabilistic controller capable of 21 effective decision making in delayed, long-horizon settings without handcrafted 22 rewards or expensive planning.

## 72 **2 Background**

73 Agents based on the world models concept extend the core idea of MBRL, learning a differentiable 74 predictive model to facilitate policy optimization and planning via *imaginations* in the model [19, 6]. 75 They create latent representations that capture spatial and temporal aspects to model dynamics and 76 predict the future [19]. The architecture governing this dynamics—generative model—and how 77 it is leveraged for policy and planning is foundational in this concept. Many designs resemble 78 variational autoencoder [20] and are often augmented with Recurrent State-Space Models (RSSMs) 79 to provide memory and help with credit assignment [21, 6, 14]. At the same time, RL methods 80 such as actor–critic [1] are integrated with the model to optimize the policy [13, 6, 14], yielding 81 sample-efficient agents that rely on imagination rather than extensive environment interaction. 82 AIF offers a complementary, neuroscience-grounded perspective that subsumes predictive coding 83 that postulates that the brain minimizes prediction errors—relative to an internal generative model 84 of the world—under uncertainty [22]. It casts the brain as a hierarchy that performs variational 85 Bayesian inference continuously to suppress prediction error [9]. It was originally advanced to 86 explain how organisms actively control and navigate their environments by iteratively updating 87 beliefs and inferring actions from sensory observations [9]. AIF emphasizes the dependency of 88 observations on actions [22]; accordingly, it posits that actions are chosen, while calibrating the 89 model, to align with preferences and reduce uncertainty, thereby unifying perception, action, and 90 learning [22]. The free-energy principle provides the mathematical bedrock for this framework 91 [23, 24], and a growing body of empirical work supports its biological plausibility [25]. AIF-based 92 agents have been deployed in robotics, autonomous driving, and clinical decision support [26, 27, 28], 93 demonstrating robust performance in uncertain, dynamic settings. In this work, we adopt the AIF 94 formulation of Fountas et al. (2020) [12], which was extended in [29, 11] and has been shown to 95 result in effective agents across different environments—such as visual and industrial tasks. We then 96 review the planning strategies that can be coupled with this formalism.

## 97 **2.1 Formalism**

98 Within AIF, agents employ an integrated probabilistic framework consisting of an internal generative 99 model [30] with inference mechanisms that allow them to represent and act upon the world. The 100 framework assumes a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process [31, 30, 32], where an agent's 101 interaction with its environment is formalized in terms of three random variables—observation, latent 102 state, and action—denoted (ot, st, at) at time t. In contrast to RL, this formalism does not rely on 103 explicit reward feedback from the environment; instead, the agent learns solely from the sequence of 104 observations it receives. ## 182 **3 Deep Active Inference Agent**

183 From habit-integrated MCTS to hybrid-horizon and gradient-based latent imagination, state-of-the-art 184 agents increasingly integrate policy learning with planning to capture the long-term effects essential 185 for scalable and sample-efficient control. A prominent approach is latent imagination, notably used by 186 Dreamer agents [6, 21, 13], which perform sequential rollouts in latent space using a RSSM. Besides 187 its computational burden, this method risks accumulating errors as networks are repeatedly inferred 188 and sampled. These models embed the policy network in the latent space by sampling actions along 189 each latent-state trajectory, so policy optimization depends on a large number of samplings in the 190 model's imaginations. 191 A simpler strategy assumes a generative model that *knows* the exact form of the policy function—in 192 other words, the network parameters themselves. We can train such a model to generate a prediction 193 deep into the horizon with a single look-ahead, once provided with the policy parameters governing 194 interaction with the environment over that horizon. Thus, the EFE can be computed directly over the 195 horizon, and its gradients can be backpropagated to minimize the EFE while still guiding the agent 196 toward its intrinsic and extrinsic objectives. Given that the policy is optimized through the gradient 197 steps of the EFE, this approach naturally scales to continuous action space rather than choosing 198 discrete actions, as in earlier AIF-agent implementations[12]. Here, we adopt this AIF-consistent 199 generative-policy modeling, without incorporating further mechanisms typically employed to further 200 enhance world models or AIF agents.

## 201 **3.1 Architecture**

202 The agent comprises, at a minimum, a **policy network** that directly interacts with the environment and 203 a **generative model** that is trained to optimize that policy. Conditioned on the policy, the generative 204 model constitutes the core of AIF and can be instantiated with various architectures. In this work we 205 adopt a generic—yet commonly used—autoencoder assembly [12] to instantiate the formalism of 206 Sec. 2.1, which requires the tightly coupled modules illustrated in Fig. ??. Leveraging amortization 207 [20, 43, 47] to scale inference [12], the generative model is parameterized by two sets: θ = {θs, θo}
for prior generation and ϕ = {ϕs} for recognition. Accordingly, the **Encoder** Qϕs 208 (st) performs 209 amortized inference by mapping the currently sampled observation o˜t to a posterior distribution 210 over the latent state st [48]. The key difference here is that, rather than sampling actions inside the latent dynamics, we incorporate a policy function—or **Actor**—Qϕa 211 (at | o˜t), which itself infers a 212 distribution over actions with parameters ϕa. We therefore introduce an explicit representation for 213 the function itself with the mapping Π : Qϕa → πˆ, resulting in πˆ(ϕa). This approach is common in 214 neural implicit representations [49]; recent work has moreover demonstrated that neural functions 215 with diverse computational graphs can be embedded efficiently [50]. Conditioned on the actor, the Transition, Pθs 216 (st+1 |s˜t, πˆ), *overshoots* the latent dynamics up to a planning horizon H, producing 217 a distribution for st+H given the sampled latent state at time t, while the actor–denoted by ϕa–is assumed fixed throughout the horizon. Finally, the **Decoder** Pθo 218 (ot+H |s˜t+H) converts the predicted 219 latent state back into a distribution over future observations. 220 Each of the three modules in the generative model is realized by a neural network that outputs the 221 parameters of a diagonal multivariate Gaussian, thereby approximating a pre-selected likelihood 222 family. They can be trained end-to-end by minimizing the VFE (Eq. 1), whereas the actor is 223 optimized—using predictions from the calibrated model—by minimizing the EFE (Eq. 4). In this 224 way, the agent unifies the two free-energy paradigms derived in the formalism. Aside from the actor 225 and transition, which account for latent dynamics with a single look-ahead, the architecture resembles 226 a variational autoencoder (VAE) [20]; nevertheless, other generative mechanisms, such as diffusion 227 or memory-based RSSM models, can be extended to support the same objective.

## 228 **3.2 Policy Optimization**

229 We propose a concise yet effective formulation for embedding the actor within the generative model 230 so that it serves as a planner that minimizes the EFE via gradient descent. Conditioned on a fixed 231 policy πˆ(ϕa), the model generates the prediction distribution Pθ(ot+H|ϕa), from which we compute 232 the EFE, denoted as the function Gθ(˜*o, ϕ*a). Policy optimization then proceeds by updating the actor 233 parameters according to the gradient ∇ϕaGθ(˜*o, ϕ*a). Most world-model agents introduce stochasticity 234 by sampling actions during imagination, which promotes exploration—typically aided by auxiliary 235 terms during the policy gradient. This results in a Monte Carlo estimation of the policy across 236 imagined trajectories, which is then differentiated based on the return [13]. In contrast, our approach 237 assumes the exact form of the policy is integrated into the dynamics, and exploration is driven by the 238 AIF formalism based on the generative model.

239 To effectively estimate the different components of the EFE in Eq. 4, Fountas et al. (2020) [12]
240 employed multiple levels of Monte Carlo sampling. While their original formulation incorporated 241 sampled actions over multi-step horizons, the same structure and sampling scheme remain beneficial 242 when using an integrated actor with deep temporal overshooting. Similarly, we adopt ancestral 243 sampling to generate the prediction Pθ(ot+H | ϕa) and leverage dropout [51] in the networks. It's 244 coupled with further sampling from the latent distributions to compute the entropies necessary for 245 calculating the EFE terms. Crucially, under the AIF framework, agents need a prior preference 246 over predictions to guide behavior—this is formalized through the extrinsic value (i.e., Eq. 4a). 247 Accordingly, we define an analytical mapping that transforms the prediction distribution into a 248 continuous preference spectrum, Ψ : Pθ(oτ ) → [0, 1].

249 Unlike RL, which relies on a monotonic return value based on accumulated rewards, this formulation 250 allows the agent to express more general and nuanced forms of preference. In practice, designing 251 a suitable reward function for RL agents remains a difficult task, often resulting in sparse or hand252 crafted signals that can be costly to design and compute. The flexibility in preference, however, 253 introduces challenges—particularly when agents have complex preference-space and must act with 254 short-sighted EFE approximations. Our approach, by optimizing planning through deep temporal 255 prediction, mitigates this issue and enables longer-term evaluation of the extrinsic value.

## 256 **3.2.1 Training & Planning**

257 During training, the generative model gradually learns how different actor parameters ϕa affect 258 the dynamics, and during policy optimization, this learned dynamics is then used to differentiate 259 the actor toward lower EFE or surprise. Critical for effective policy learning is the accuracy of 260 the world model, which forms the foundation of AIF [23, 9, 12] and predictive coding [22]. To 261 improve model training, we introduce experience replay [4] using a memory buffer M, from which 262 we sample batches of experiences, while ensuring that each batch includes the most recent transition.

263 We compute the VFE in Eq. 1 for these experiences to train the model with β-regularization. With 264 the updated model, we differentiate the EFE over a batch of observations—including previous and 265 current ones—within imagined trajectories of length H, training the actor similarly to world-model 266 methods [13, 6, 19]. This results in a joint training algorithm 1 that alternates between updating 267 the generative model and the policy, using the model to guide planning via policy gradients. This 268 approach, policy learning—rather than explicit action planning—relaxes the bounded-sight constraint 269 of EFE, as the policy is iteratively trained across diverse scenarios within the planning horizon, and 270 its effective *sight* extends beyond the nominal horizon H. Recent work on AIF-based agents has 271 also emphasized the advantages of integrating a policy network with the EFE objective [14]. After 272 training concludes and the agent's model is fixed, the agent can still leverage its model for planning.

273 Specifically, EFE-based gradient updates can be applied at the observation level once every H steps, 274 effectively fine-tuning the policy for the immediate horizon.

## 294 **4.1 Application**

295 We focus on simulating workstations in an automotive manufacturing system composed of parallel, 296 identical machines (see Appendix for details). Control actions—switching machines on or off—aim to improve energy efficiency without 301 compromising throughput. Due to stochastic delays, the system connects continuous-time dynamics 302 to discrete-time decisions, making performance only observable over long horizons. Accordingly, 303 we employ a window-based preference metric [11] that evaluates KPIs over the past eight hours.

## 313 **4.2 Results**

314 To validate the performance of our agent in the aforementioned environment, we adopted a rigorous 315 evaluation scheme based on Algorithm 1. Unlike previous works that used batch interactions to 316 improve training efficiency [12], our agent was trained in each epoch by interacting with a single 317 environment instance, reflecting a more challenging setting. The trained agent's performance was 318 then evaluated across several randomly initialized environments. From these, the best-performing 319 instance was selected for a one-month simulation run to assess energy efficiency and production loss, 320 in comparison to a baseline scenario where no control was applied and machines were continuously 321 active. We also constructed a compositional preference score—analogous to a reward function—based 322 on time-window KPIs for energy consumption and production, serving as an overall indicator of agent 323 performance, which is part of the observation of the agent. To enforce further regularization in the 324 latent space to match a normal distribution, we used a *Sigmoid* function in its non-saturated domain. 325 Since we needed to encode the actor function, which is essentially a computational graph [50], we 326 adopted a simple, non-parametric mapping Π that concatenates the input with the first hidden and 327 output values. Given its input-output structure and the fact that the model was continuously trained 328 with that, this mapping effectively serves as an approximation of the actor's neural function (see 329 Appendix for details on the agent and experimental setup). 330 We implemented the agent in the exact production system, using parameters verified to reflect realistic 331 conditions, following the aforementioned scheme. Figure 1 presents the performance of the agent 332 with an overshooting horizon of H = 300. During evaluations after each epoch (100 iterations), 333 the agent improved the preference score of observations (Fig. 1a), which correlates with increased 334 energy efficiency (Fig. 1b). Notably, the EEF of imagined trajectories used for policy updates 335 decreased as the agent learned to control the system. This trend is observed in both the extrinsic and 336 uncertainty components of the EFE. Since policy optimization relies heavily on learning a robust 337 generative model—with the actor integrated within it—the agent gradually improved its predictive 338 capacity and reduced reconstruction error across both discrete (Fig. 1d, preference) and continuous 339 (Fig. 1e,f, machine and buffer states) elements of the observation space. While EFE and overall 340 performance eventually stabilized, the generative model continued to improve, indicating that full 341 reconstruction of future observations is not strictly required for effective control. The agent manages 342 to improve the performance even when the overshooting horizon can be longer (e.g., H = 1000 steps; 343 see Appendix). We then evaluated the trained agent over one month of simulated interaction (10 344 replications), applying gradient updates every H steps during planning. Loffredo et al. (2023) [57] 345 tested model-free RL agents across a reward parameter ϕ , with DQN emerging as the top performer. 346 Table1 shows that our DAIF agent outstrips this baseline, raising energy efficiency per production 347 unit by 10.21% ± 0.14% while keeping throughput loss negligible.

359 **Limitations and future work:** While predicting an H-step transition removes the expensive *per-step* 360 planning loop, the agent still has to gather *experience* after H interactions and store it in the replay 361 buffer for training, so its sample-efficiency can still be improved. To update the world model after 362 each new environment interaction—obtained under different actor/moving parameters—we need an 363 operator that aggregates the *sequence* of actor representations. Recurrent models are a natural choice 364 for this, but their sequential unrolling adds latency and can hinder gradient flow. A lighter alternative 365 is to treat the H embeddings as an (almost) unordered set and use a set function [59]; when the 366 temporal structure with simple positional embeddings (e.g. sinusoidal [60]) can be concatenated 367 before the set pooling. This allows us to break the horizon into segments—down to a single 368 step—and still backpropagate EFE gradients during planning through the aggregations the current 369 policy representation. Finally, (neural) operator-learning techniques could enable resolution-invariant 370 aggregation across function spaces [61, 62]. Additional extensions include replacing the VAE world 371 model with diffusion- or flow-matching-based generators [28], adopting actor–critic optimization 372 (as in Dreamer and related world-model agents [13, 6, 14]), and introducing regularization schemes 373 to stabilize EFE gradient updates and reduce their variance. Rapid adaptation in non-stationary 374 settings—where model-free agents often struggle—remains an especially promising direction.

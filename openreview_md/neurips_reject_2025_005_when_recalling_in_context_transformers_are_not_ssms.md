## Abstract

1 Despite the advantageous subquadratic complexity of modern recurrent deep learn2 ing models - such as state-space models (SSMs) - recent studies have highlighted 3 their potential shortcomings compared to transformers on reasoning and memoriza4 tion tasks. In this paper, we dive deeper into one of such benchmarks: associative 5 recall (AR), which has been shown to correlate well with language modeling 6 performance, and inspect in detail the effects of scaling and optimization issues 7 in recently proposed token mixing strategies. We first demonstrate that, unlike 8 standard transformers, the choice of learning rate plays a critical role in the per9 formance of modern recurrent models: an issue that can severely affect reported 10 performance in previous works and suggests further research is needed to stabilize 11 training. Next, we show that recurrent and attention-based models exhibit contrast12 ing benefits when scaling in width as opposed to depth, with attention being notably 13 unable to solve AR when limited to a single layer. We then further inspect 1-layer 14 transformers, revealing that despite their poor performance, their training dynamics 15 surprisingly resemble the formation of induction heads, a phenomenon previously 16 observed only in their 2-layer counterparts. Finally, through architectural ablations, 17 we study how components affects Transformer and Mamba's performance and 18 optimization stability.

## 19 **1 Introduction**

20 Since early developments [Rumelhart et al., 1986, Elman, 1990], RNNs have driven progress in 21 machine learning techniques for sequential data, with milestones such as Echo-State Networks [Jaeger, 22 2001] the LSTM [Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997] and the GRU [Cho et al., 2014]. However, two 23 problems severely limit the application of RNNs in modern times: first, GPU architectures designed 24 for large matrix multiplications struggle with sequential processing. Secondly, it is widely known 25 that recurrent models are hard to train due to vanishing and exploding gradients issues [Bengio et al., 26 1994, Hochreiter et al., 2001, Pascanu et al., 2013].

27 **Attention.** These challenges have led to the introduction of a different paradigm: the Attention 28 mechanism, implemented around the Transformer architecture [Vaswani et al., 2017]. Instead of 29 processing inputs sequentially while building up internal memory (RNNs), Attention computes 30 large matrices of pairwise interactions between data points, allowing for modeling direct links 31 between elements in a sequence and thus attenuating the vanishing gradient issue. While Attention, 32 being based on matrix multiplications, is extremely GPU efficient, computing pairwise interactions results in O(L
2 33 ) inference and memory complexity, where L denotes the input sequence length. For 34 this reason, techniques such as patching [Dosovitskiy et al., 2021, Pagnoni et al., 2024], gradient 35 checkpointing [Chen et al., 2016], and FlashAttention [Dao et al., 2022, Dao, 2023, Shah et al., 36 2024] become of paramount importance when training and deploying Attention-based models at 37 scale. Despite this limitation, Transformers successfully powers most state-of-the-art architectures 38 we use today: beyond large language models [Devlin, 2018, Brown et al., 2020, Team et al., 2024],
39 Attention found widespread application in vision [Dosovitskiy et al., 2021, Touvron et al., 2021, 40 Bertasius et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2024a], graph processing [Ma et al., 2023], and genome analysis 41 domains [Dalla-Torre et al., 2024], among others. 42 Nevertheless, the quadratic complexity of Attention has remained a pressing limitation, prompting 43 numerous efforts to develop more efficient approximations [Wang et al., 2020, Choromanski et al., 44 2020, Chen et al., 2021, Lee-Thorp et al., 2022]. Many of these approaches have even revealed 45 connections to recurrent formulations [Katharopoulos et al., 2020, Schlag et al., 2021]. 46 **SSMs and other linear token mixers.** More recently, we have witnessed a resurgence of RNNs in 47 state-of-the-art industry-size applications such as language modeling. Sparked by the S4 model [Gu 48 et al., 2020, 2022], which surpassed Attention-based models on long-range reasoning tasks [Tay 49 et al., 2020], we have rapidly seen in the last year a drastic increase in the usage of RNNs in deep architectures, albeit in a linear1 50 form that guarantees both O(L) memory/inference complexity 51 and fast computation on GPUs [Martin and Cundy, 2018, Orvieto et al., 2023] while matching or 52 surpassing transformers on downstream tasks: a prime example are State-space Models (SSMs) 53 such as Mamba(2) [Gu and Dao, 2024, Dao and Gu, 2024], along with variants based on similar 54 ambitions [De et al., 2024, Peng et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024a]. These novel fast recurrent processing 55 strategies sparked the interest of many practitioners in the field, leading to novel applications in 56 several domains, including vision [Liu et al., 2024b, Liang et al., 2024], audio generation [Goel et al., 57 2022], online learning [Zucchet et al., 2023] and reinforcement learning [Lu et al., 2023]. 58 **Different expressivity?** It has been shown [Dao and Gu, 2024, Ali et al., 2024, Sieber et al., 2024], 59 that one can put in direct correspondence Attention with SSM processing: due to the linearity of 60 SSMs in the hidden state - the main distinction between SSMs and classical nonlinear RNNs [Cirone 61 et al., 2024] - it is possible to write down the "attention matrix" corresponding to SSM processing 62 at a given input. Yet, such a matrix is highly structured, a feature that boosts speeds at very high 63 context lengths [Waleffe et al., 2024] but may hurt optimization due to iterated products involving the 64 model parameters [Zucchet and Orvieto, 2024]. On top of this, the recurrent formulation of SSMs 65 clearly points to potential memory issues compared to attention on simple yet important tasks such as 66 copying [Jelassi et al., 2024] and associative recall [Arora et al., 2023]. 67 **.. or perhaps just harder optimization?** Despite empirical results and worst-case bounds [Arora 68 et al., 2024] regarding the capabilities of SSM models on simple yet important tasks, it is yet unclear 69 if optimization issues, such as the curse of memory or vanishing gradients [Pascanu et al., 2013, 70 Zucchet and Orvieto, 2024], confound or understanding of capabilities of new recurrent models. We 71 found that this is indeed the case in associative recall [Arora et al., 2023], as we show in Figure 1. 72 Figure 1 points to a crucial confounder when comparing SSM and attention capabilities: while funda73 mental expressivity issues exist between such model classes, the main driver of poor performance can 74 be unsuccessful optimization. In a way, when considering basic prototypical yet challenging tasks, 75 *transformers are not SSMs* **mainly because of their optimization dynamics.** 76 In response to this issue, a potential confounder for future evaluations of new recurrent models, in 77 this paper we take a closer look at associative recall and reveal new insights into the fundamental 78 distinctions between attention models and recurrent models.

## 171 **3 Closer Look Into Ar Performance**

172 Building on previous research, we aim to provide an in-depth analysis of the differences and simi173 larities between attention and recurrent models through the lens of AR. Prior studies [Arora et al., 174 2023] have shown that transformers are inherently well-suited for solving the MQAR task, achieving 175 perfect accuracy regardless of model dimension, sequence length or number of key-value pairs to 176 infer. In contrast, it was argued (both theoretically and empirically) that new recurrent models [Peng 177 et al., 2023, Nguyen et al., 2024, Gu and Dao, 2024] can only solve MQAR if the hidden dimension 178 is roughly equal to the sequence length (see analysis by Jelassi et al. [2024] in a related setting). 179 However, a key aspect that has been **overlooked** in some prior works is the crucial role of optimization 180 in recurrent models —particularly, the use of an effective grid search for the choice of learning rate. 181 **Hypothesis from previous works.** Recurrent models update their hidden state (which serves as a 182 compressed representation of past information) at each time step, using the current input. Since the 183 model only has access to its hidden state and the current input, its ability to recall previous information 184 depends on how effectively it compresses past data into this state. With a simplified analysis assuming 185 uniform distribution over strings, Jelassi et al. [2024] showed that to successfully copy input strings, 186 the hidden size needed grows linearly with the sequence length. In contrast, transformers [Vaswani 187 et al., 2017] dynamically access all previously seen inputs through the softmax attention mechanism, 188 allowing for the explicit computation of interactions between tokens. This makes the task of recalling 189 already seen tokens essentially a lookup table problem when two layers work simultaneously, as 190 described in Jelassi et al. [2024], Olsson et al. [2022]. 191 **Results.** Compared to previous work, in our experiments, we devoted more attention to tuning the 192 learning rates, drastically improving the reported performance for recurrent models (see Fig. 2&1). 193 As shown in Figure 2 and extensively in Appendix A.3, a finer grid not only enhances average 194 performance across all settings but also proves particularly crucial for the Mamba model. With a more 195 suitable learning rate, Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2024], which was previously shown to struggle with 196 long sequence lengths, becomes capable of solving MQAR at relatively small hidden model sizes. 197 All experimental details for this and the next experiments are in Appendix A.2. This highlights a key 198 takeaway for MQAR: the choice of learning rate (and optimization strategy in general) can be decisive 199 in assessing whether a recurrent model can solve the task at all. In the case of Mamba, optimization 200 choices become a discriminative factor, emphasizing the necessity of careful hyperparameter tuning 201 in recurrent models, and further research for improving their high sensitivity. 202 To further emphasize the critical role of learning rate selection in training recurrent models, we 203 compare the performance of Attention, Hyena and Mamba using the same grid search. Figure 1 204 illustrates that attention-based models maintain strong performance across a relatively wide range of 205 learning rates. In contrast, Hyena and Mamba exhibit a different behavior: performance remains near 206 zero for most learning rates but suddenly reaches near-optimal levels at specific values which may 207 not be included in the grid by Arora et al. [2023]. These findings highlight a key distinction between 208 attention-based and recurrent models: a sparse learning rate grid search can disproportionately impact 209 their training outcomes. **This discrepancy can lead to misleading conclusions** about the capabilities 210 of these models, emphasizing the need for careful tuning.

## 270 **6 Are Ssms And Transformers Really Similar?**

271 Our results so far highlight key differences between Transformers and SSMs, particularly Mamba, in 272 the context of AR. Notably, while Mamba demonstrates greater expressivity—successfully solving 273 the task even in a single-layer setting—it presents optimization challenges in terms of learning 274 rate stability. In contrast, Transformers exhibit remarkable stability across a wide range of suitable 275 learning rates during training in the 2-layer setting. We highlight that each of these layers includes an 276 MLP block processing channels. 277 To address this discrepancy, we conduct a series of ablation studies aimed at: 278 1. Modifying the architectures of both models to better align them (architectures are in appendix 279 A.1) and identify the source of Mamba's superior performance, summarized in Table 1 and 280 2. Exploring new architectural variants that promote more stable training dynamics. 281 **Convolutions.** Inspired by [Li et al., 2024], we begin by adapting the attention mechanism 282 to resemble Mamba. We incorporate a 1D convolution before the Query,Key and Value matrix 283 projections to brings in locality, enabling the model to solve MQAR with just one layer. Interestingly, 284 we observe that applying the convolution to either the Key or Value matrix alone is sufficient to 285 achieve the same performance gains. These observations suggest that the 1D convolution may be 286 a central factor behind Mamba's effectiveness. However, we find that even after removing the 287 convolution, the model retains the ability to solve the task. 288 **Backbone ablation.** We further modify the Mamba architecture to make it closer to the Transformer 289 architecture by: (1) removing the gating mechanism, and (2) replacing the standard Mamba block 290 with a sequence mixer (as in S6), followed by an MLP—mirroring the Transformer's architecture.

291 Despite these alterations, Mamba continues to perform well when properly tuned, suggesting a 292 notable degree of robustness in the design of its fundamental block.

293 **Newer architectures.** To better understand what contributes to training stability, we also evaluate 294 architectural variants designed for improving the Mamba architecture and solve the MQAR task. 295 In particular, we test Mamba2 [Dao and Gu, 2024] and DeltaNet [Yang et al., 2024b] as shown 296 in Figure 6. While performance of Mamba2 is slightly more stable, Transformer-level robustness 297 is only achieved by DeltaNet. A closer look at the DeltaNet update rule reveals that its mixing is based on Householder matrices. As such, the off-diagonal terms such as CNQN
298 k=1 AkB0 do not 299 necessarily incur in vanishing gradients. Instead, in both Mamba and Mamba2, Ak includes a decay 300 rate that induces vanishing gradients and fast decay of off-diagonal terms, as recently pointed out 301 by Trockman et al. [2024]. We hypothesize this is the main distinction unlocking stable optimization 302 in DeltaNet.

## 303 **7 Discussion And Conclusions**

304 In this work, we used MQAR as a benchmark to compare attention and recurrent models at a 305 small scale. Our findings shed additional light on how the underlying mechanisms of these models 306 influence their performance. Specifically, we showed that recurrent models are highly sensitive 307 to optimization, with their performance significantly affected by the choice of learning rate. This 308 underscores the need for further research to improve their stability. Additionally, we observed 309 contrasting scaling behaviors: recurrent models benefit from the increased width and hidden state 310 size, whereas transformers struggle with MQAR in a single-layer configuration. Interestingly, despite 311 their poor performance, single-layer transformers exhibit training dynamics resembling the induction 312 head phenomenon, previously reported only in multi-layer settings. Instead, Mamba displays similar 313 behavior but successfully solves the task. Finally, through the ablations study, we showed how the 314 performance of Mamba is robust to specific architectural components such as gating and convolution, 315 and how other similar architectures can enhance performance and stability. Our findings suggest 316 overlaps between the optimization landscapes of Mamba and Attention, yet with crucial differences 317 related to expressivity, to study further. Looking ahead, we think that exploring other synthetic 318 reasoning tasks and architectural changes could provide further insights into the mechanisms behind 319 these models. Evaluating these areas is an important direction to refine our understanding of modern 320 sequence models.

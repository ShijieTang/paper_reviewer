## Abstract

1 The greatest ambition of mechanistic interpretability is to completely rewrite deep 2 neural networks in a format that is more amenable to human understanding, while 3 preserving their behavior and performance. In this paper we evaluate whether 4 sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and transcoders can be used for this purpose. We use 5 an automated pipeline to generate explanations for each of the sparse coder latents. 6 We then simulate the activation of each latent on a number of different inputs using 7 an LLM prompted with the explanation we generated in the previous step, and 8 "partially rewrite" the original model by patching the simulated activations into 9 its forward pass. We find that current sparse coding techniques and automated 10 interpretability pipelines are not up to the task of rewriting even a single layer 11 of a transformer: the model is severely degraded by patching in the simulated 12 activations. We believe this approach is the most thorough way to assess the quality 13 of SAEs and transcoders, despite its high computational cost. 15 While large language models (LLMs) have reached human level performance in many areas [Guo et al., 16 2025], we understand little about their internal representations. Early mechanistic interpretability 17 research attempted to explain the activation patterns of individual neurons [Olah et al., 2020, Gurnee 18 et al., 2023, 2024], but research has found that most neurons are "polysemantic", activating in 19 semantically diverse contexts [Arora et al., 2018, Elhage et al., 2022]. 20 Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) were proposed to address polysemanticity [Cunningham et al., 2023]. 21 SAEs consist of two parts: an encoder that transforms activation vectors into a sparse, higher22 dimensional latent space, and a decoder that projects the latents back into the original space. Both 23 parts are trained jointly to minimize reconstruction error. Recently, a significant effort was made to 24 scale SAE training to larger models, like GPT-4 [Gao et al., 2024] and Claude 3 Sonnet [Templeton 25 et al., 2024], and they have become an important interpretability tool for LLMs. Recently Dunefsky 26 et al. [2024] proposed sparse *transcoders* as an alternative method for extracting interpretable features 27 from LLMs. The architecture of the transcoder is identical to that of an SAE, but it is trained to 28 predict the output of a feedforward network given its input. With a good enough transcoder, we 29 should be able to entirely replace the original FFN with its transcoder approximation, thereby partially 30 rewriting the model in terms of more interpretable primitives. 31 While it seems that neurons and sparse autoencoder latents can be explained by looking at the 32 examples they activate on [Bills et al., 2023], some are more easily understood by their downstream 33 effects [Gur-Arieh et al., 2025]. Paulo et al. [2024] took inspiration on Bills et al. [2023] and built 34 an automated pipeline for generating natural language explanations of SAE latents and evaluating 35 how good these explanations are. Rigorously measuring how interpretable an explanation is still a 36 complicated and methodologically fraught task.

37 The idea of rewriting a neural net in a more interpretable form is not new. The "microscope AI"
38 framework [Hubinger, 2019] aims to analyze a neural network's learned representations to gain 39 actionable insights for humans, rather than using the network directly. These insights would likely 40 take the form of natural language explanations of the network's features and circuits. Microscope 41 AI aims to reduce risks associated with model deployment while still benefiting from the model's 42 knowledge. Imitative generalization is a proposal to extend this idea by jointly optimizing the network 43 and its human-interpretable annotations to maximize their prior likelihood [Barnes, 2021]. Sparse 44 autoencoders and similar techniques can then be used as "explainer" models, explaining the behavior 45 of the network by sparsely decomposing its activations. 46 In this work, we pursue the following idea: if the latents of a transcoder are interpretable enough, 47 we can *simulate* its activations using natural language explanations. Specifically, we replace the 48 encoder of the transcoder with an LLM prompted to predict the activation of each latent given its 49 explanation and the textual context. We then patch this modified transcoder back into the model, 50 hopefully yielding behavior nearly identical to the unpatched model. In the limit, we could use this to 51 "rewrite" every feedforward layer in the model in terms of interpretable features and operations on 52 those features. 53 Unfortunately there are several roadblocks for this approach. Firstly, despite a majority latents 54 seeming to have interpretable activations, a large fraction is either uninterpretable or not well55 captured by our current pipeline. Secondly, even the ones that seem interpretable are hard to explain 56 in their totality, as it is hard to generate simple explanations that are both contextually sensitive 57 and specific. Finally, the predictions of the simulator LLM are not calibrated: it over-estimates the 58 frequency with which latents fire, and how strong their activations should be. Because we believe 59 that there are many potential ways to improve the quality and specificity of explanations, we see this 60 test as a potential benchmark of transcoder and SAE interpretability.

## 61 **2 Methods** 62 **2.1 Sparse Coder Training**

63 We train different types of sparse coders to evaluate their potential for partial rewriting. We begin by 64 training a skip-transcoder on the MLP of the sixth layer of Pythia 160M [Biderman et al., 2023] as 65 well as a skip-transcoder on the MLP of the fifteenth layer of SmolLM2 135M [Allal et al., 2025]. We 66 also train a sparse autoencoder on the output of the 6th layer of Pythia 160M. The skip-transcoders 67 have a linear "skip connection," which Paulo et al. [2025] found improves the ability to approximate 68 the original MLP at no cost to interpretability scores. That is, the transcoder takes the functional form yˆ = W2TopK(W1x + b1) + Wskipx + b2 (1)
69 where x is the input of the MLP and yˆ is the reconstructed output of the MLP. Both W2 and Wskip 70 are zero-initialized, and b2 is initialized to the empirical mean of the MLP outputs, so that the 71 transcoder is a constant function at the beginning of training. All models are trained to minimize the mean squared error ||y − yˆ||2 72 2 between the model's output and the target module output.

## 83 **2.2 Interpreting Latents**

84 We use the automated interpretability pipeline released by Paulo et al. [2024] to generate explanations 85 and scores for transcoder and SAE latents. For all sparse coders we collected activations over 10M
86 tokens of the same dataset they were trained on. For each latent, representative samples of its 87 activations are sampled and shown to an LLM, in our case Llama 3.1 70b Instruct [Dubey et al., 88 2024], which is told to give a succinct explanation that summarizes the activations (Figure 1, first 89 row). We only explain latents that are active on more than 200 instances over the 10M samples taken. 90 The LLM is shown 40 examples, 4 examples from each of the 10 activation deciles for that latent. 91 After explanations are generated for each latent, they are scored. We also use the automated 92 interpretability pipeline from Paulo et al. [2024] for this process. We use both fuzzing and detection 93 to score the latents. To compute the detection score, Llama 3.1 70b Instruct is given the explanation 94 of the latent and a set of examples. The LLM then has to decide which examples activate the latent 95 and which don't using the explanation that was given. We repeat this process 20 times, showing 96 five examples each time, totaling 50 non-activating examples and 50 activating examples. We use 97 stratified random sampling from the 10 activating deciles of that latent. At the end the detection score of the latent is given by the F1 score of the scorer, F1 =2 98p−1+r−1 , where p is the precision and r is 99 the recall. The fuzzing score is computed with a similar protocol, but instead the LLM is shown a set 100 examples where one token is highlighted in each, and the model has to label each highlighted token 101 as activating or non-activating. Again the final metric used is the F1 score.

## 102 **2.3 Simulation**

103 The next step in the pipeline is to use an LLM to simulate the activation of a latent in a context given 104 the latent's explanation (Figure 1, middle panel). For this task, we select up to 10K sequences, each 105 32 tokens long, and highlight the final one. The LLM is tasked to output a single number from 0 to 9 106 to quantify the activation of the latent on that token for that specific sequence. Because our scorer 107 model has a token for each independent digit, we can estimate the expected value by summing the 108 probability of each digit times the value of the digit. We then map this number to the real activations 109 by dividing the expected value by nine (yielding a number between 0 and 1), then multiplying this 110 value by the maximum observed activation for the latent. The prompt used for simulation can be 111 found in Appendix A.

## 112 **2.3.1 Quantile Normalization** 125 **3 Evaluation**

126 We evaluate the rewriting of the model by measuring the resulting cross-entropy loss in next token 127 prediction on chunks of text sampled from the Pile. These chunks of texts have lengths that are 128 uniformly sampled from 32 to 256 tokens. This range was selected such that, at its minimum, there is 129 a significant number of tokens that can be shown to the simulator model, and at its maximum the 130 sequences are short enough that the presence of very long range features is unlikely. 131 Simply replacing a single MLP with a transcoder increases the model's cross-entropy loss by about 132 10%. This loss increase is equivalent to that of using an early Pythia checkpoint, one that was trained on only 25% of the data,3 133 rather than the final Pythia checkpoint. Rewriting any part of the transcoder 134 in natural language will necessarily degrade the model's performance even further. Consequently, 135 we focus on rewriting a single MLP block, since rewriting all MLP blocks simultaneously would 136 cause the model to become completely unusable. The same applies to the residual stream SAE: the 137 cross-entropy loss of the model when adding a single SAE to the residual stream increases by 33%. 138 This is equivalent to using a Pythia checkpoint trained on less than 10% of the data. 139 After collecting the predicted activations for all features of a given token in a given sentence, and 140 after applying quantile normalization to those predictions, we use the TopK activation function to 141 ensure it has the expected level of sparsity. This activation vector is fed into the decoder, yielding a 142 reconstructed output vector. In the case of the transcoders, the original output of the MLP is discarded 143 and in its place we inject the reconstructed vector (Figure 1), while in the case of the SAE the output 144 of the layer is discarded and in its place the reconstructed vector is patched in. 145 We evaluate over 10K different contexts for the Pythia transcoder, 2K for the SmolLM2 transcoder, 146 and 1K for the Pythia SAE, measuring the cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction. This process 147 is very expensive, as each context requires individually prompting the simulator for 32 thousand 148 latents. We therefore had to make a total of 327 million queries to the simulator model. Our simulator 149 models were hosted locally using VLLM [Kwon et al., 2023], and the experiments took 1 week on 4 150 nodes with 8xA40 GPUs. 151 **Partial rewriting.** We also experiment with mixing predicted and ground truth latent activations in 152 varying proportions, allowing us to examine the effect of rewriting only part of the encoder. We do 153 this in two different ways:
154 1. Select the top k most interpretable features according to our evaluation pipeline [Paulo et al.,
155 2024]. This is labeled "Top scoring" in Figure 2. 156 2. Sample k features uniformly at random from the transcoder. This is labeled "Sampling" in 157 Figure 2. 113 We found in early experiments that Llama produces highly uncalibrated predictions of feature 114 activations: the marginal distribution of the predicted activations differs markedly from the marginal 115 distribution of the true activations (Figure 3, left panel). Patching these uncalibrated activations 116 into the model yields very poor results. To alleviate this problem, we use quantile normalization, 117 which monotonically transforms the model's predictions in such a way that their marginal distribution 118 matches that of the true activations. This transformation is an optimal transport map under a variety 119 of cost functions [Santambrogio, 2015]. 120 We compute the quantile normalizer separately for each individual feature, using the empirical 121 CDFs of the simulator's predicted activations and the true activations of a transcoder computed 122 from a random sample of 10% of the data. Once the quantile normalizer has been computed, this 123 transformation is then applied to all simulator predictions. This transformation successfully reduces 124 the number of predicted active latents to numbers that are coherent with the true distribution.

## 160 **4 Results**

161 The left panel of Figure 2 illustrates how cross-entropy loss increases on Pythia as we replace more 162 and more transcoder latents with their simulated counterparts. Unfortunately, we see that the "Top 163 Scoring" and "Sampling" conditions both result in very similar loss increases to the baseline "Mean 164 Ablation" condition, for most fractions of substitution. The same behavior can be seen when using 165 the residual stream SAE (Figure 2, right panel), and when using the transcoder trained on SmolLM 166 2 (Figure A2). For some fractions and models we observe that randomly selecting which latents to 167 substitute, instead of always substituting the most interpretable latents, leads to a smaller increase in 168 the cross entropy loss, showing that the interpretability metrics are somewhat capturing how easy it 169 is to describe the behaviour of the latents. While our substitution method works better than mean 170 ablation for some fractions, it is not consistently better and in fact when we do full substitution we 171 find that there is almost no difference between the two.

172 This indicates that our explanation pipeline is incapable of rewriting even a small fraction of a 173 transformer without causing a substantial increase in the next-token prediction loss– an increase 174 that is comparable to simply replacing neurons in the transformer with their mean values. This is a 175 negative result which suggests more work needs to be done to improve explanation quality.

## 176 **4.1 Explanations Are Not Detailed Enough**

177 One of the reasons that we need to calibrate the activation predictions is because we find that current 178 explanations do not allow the model to be specific enough. To understand this, let's consider the 179 case that the classifier only achieves a specificity of 99%. The transcoder used in this work has 180 32768 latents, and if the LLM predictor can only achieve a specificity of 99% this means that, that on 181 average, it predicts that there are 320 active latents, 10 times more than the actual number (k = 32).

182 Even with a sensitivity of 100%, where the model correctly identifies all of the latents that should be 183 active, it is unlikely that the top 32 predictions all would be the correct ones. We observe than on 184 average the current automatic latent explanation setup has a specificity of around 80%, meaning that 185 on average the model identifies close to 6 thousand latents as being active. 186 By performing quantile normalization, a large chunk of incorrectly predicted activations, activations 187 that should be zero but were given a non-zero value by the predictor, are set back to zero. This 188 significantly increases the specificity, enough that some of the original model's performance is 189 maintained, but at the same time this significantly decreases the sensitivity, as some of the correctly 190 classified active latents are also set to zero. 191 We find that detection scores [Paulo et al., 2024, page 5] are predictive of the specificity and 192 sensitivity of an explanation, with higher scoring latents corresponding to explanations that have 193 higher specificity and sensitivity (Figure 4). This is expected, as detection scoring corresponds to 194 detecting whether a given latent is active on a given context, which is similar to our simulation task 195 in this work. For the same reason, latents with higher fuzzing scores also have higher sensitivity and 196 specificity (Figure A1).

## 197 **5 Limitations**

198 We divide the limitations of this work into three categories. One type of limitation is related with the 199 quality of sparse coders we investigated. We only explored TopK sparse coders, trained on one to ten 200 billion tokens, with an expansion factor of 32x. However, we don't see any evidence in the literature 201 that other activation functions would make the sparse coders more interpretable, so it is unlikely that 202 other architectures would significantly improve our results. Larger sparse coders would lead to lower 203 reconstruction losses but would require even higher specificity. 204 The quality of explanations is a limitation of our work, as it is possible that better explanation 205 generation techniques could yield better rewriting results. While the quality of explanations could 206 likely be improved with careful prompt tuning or even finetuning the explainer model, we think 207 the results would not substantially change due to the huge gap between the specificity of current 208 explanations and the specificity that would be required for satisfactory model rewriting. 209 Rewriting is also very computationally expensive. Larger models would probably be able to simulate 210 the activations more accurately, but this would make the process even more expensive.

## 211 **6 Conclusion**

212 In this work, we proposed a new methodology for rigorously evaluating the faithfulness of natural 213 language explanations of sparse coder latents in transformers, based on partially rewriting the 214 transformer using these explanations. We found that existing explanations are severely wanting. We 215 are still unable to outperform mean ablation, where each latent is substituted by its average value 216 over the dataset. 217 This is mainly due to the fact that explanations are not specific enough, leading to a high number of 218 false positives. Our results highlight the fact that it is important for an explanation to correctly identify 219 the contexts where a feature is not active, in addition to the feature's activation level in contexts where 220 it is active. Future work on the interpretability of latents should take this into consideration. 221 To improve upon these results, new techniques are needed to make explanations more specific, for 222 instance using contrast pairs of highly similar features to bring out additional details. This could 223 potentially increase the sensitivity as well, which takes a big hit when using quantile normalization.

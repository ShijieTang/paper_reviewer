Despite its simplicity, this approach improves both convergence efficiency and final 35 performance. By solving this 37 nested optimization objective, the SFT updates are explicitly conditioned on the RL solution, allowing 38 SFT to provide more targeted guidance to RL. ## 166 **3.3 Explanations Of Update Rules**

167 **What does the lower-level update do?** The update rule for θ in E.q. (7) is a convex combination of 168 the SFT and RL gradients. As λ increases from 0 to 1 during training, the algorithm gradually shifts 169 from imitation learning to reinforcement learning. 170 This curriculum learning–like transition [1] is meaningful: in the early training stages, the base 171 model lacks strong reasoning abilities and benefits more from imitating expert reasoning patterns. As 172 training progresses, the model becomes capable of generating the correct answers by exploring the 173 reward signal , making RL updates more valuable. 174 **What does the higher-level update do?** The update for w in Eq. (8) aims to solve the bilevel 175 formulation in Eq. (4). Specifically, it seeks a LoRA module w such that, after training the base parameter θ on DRL, the resulting fine-tuned model θ

## 184 **4 Experiment** 185 **4.1 Settings**

186 **Datasets.** We adopt the LIMR dataset [18] for RL training, which is derived from MATH [10]. 187 Following their setup, we use the *Hard* subset (problems with MATH difficulty levels 3–5), which 188 contains approximately 1.3k problems. For the SFT dataset, we follow the procedure used in 189 DeepMath-103k [9] and distill intermediate expert reasoning steps using the R1 model. 190 For evaluation, we use the MATH500 subset as the primary test set and uniformly sample an additional 191 500 problems for validation. To assess generalization, we evaluate on a diverse set of mathematical 192 reasoning benchmarks, including MATH500 [10], Minerva Math [16], and OlympiadBench [8], as 193 well as competition-level datasets such as AIME 2024 and AMC 2023. 194 **Models.** We conduct zero-shot RL training experiments using Qwen-2.5 models [30], selected for 195 their demonstrated stability on mathematical reasoning tasks. The 3B model is used, with prompt 196 formats consistent with SimpleRL. 197 **Reward.** In line with SimpleRL-Zoo [31], we adopt a binary reward function based solely on 198 answer correctness: correct final answers receive a reward of +1, while incorrect answers receive 0. 199 We deliberately exclude format-based rewards, which have been shown to constrain exploration and 200 reduce final performance, particularly for base models.

201 **Implementation Details.** All models are trained using the verl framework [27] with unified 202 hyperparameters: a prompt batch size of 64, 5 rollouts per prompt, a maximum rollout length of 3000 203 tokens, and a mini-batch size of 64. For evaluation, we use greedy decoding with a temperature of 0 and a maximum generation length of 5000 tokens. The learning rate is set to 5 × 10−7 204 , and for LoRA, 205 both the rank and α are set to 16. The weighting coefficient λ is set to 0.5. Following SimpleRL-Zoo 206 [31], we report pass@1 accuracy for most benchmarks. For AIME 2024, due to the limited number 207 of test cases, we additionally report average accuracy over 8 samples (avg@8). All experiments are 208 conducted on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80GB).

## 209 **4.2 Baselines**

210 We evaluate our approach against a comprehensive set of baselines, all built on the same base 211 architecture. These comparisons are designed to isolate the specific contributions of our proposed 212 bilevel optimization framework. 213 **Base / Instruct Model.** The original performance of base model and its instruction version, without 214 further reasoning-specific training. This serves as a lower-bound reference for evaluating reasoning 215 capabilities.

216 **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).** A model trained solely via supervised fine-tuning on curated 217 chain-of-thought (CoT) data, without any reinforcement learning. This highlights the benefits and 218 limitations of pure imitation-based learning. 219 **RL-Zero.** Reinforcement learning applied directly to the base model without any prior fine-tuning. 220 This baseline evaluates the effectiveness of exploration from scratch, without initialization from 221 expert demonstrations. 222 **Cold-Start** A two-stage pipeline where SFT is used to pretrain the model, followed by RL fine223 tuning. The two phases are fully decoupled, with no interaction between supervised and reward-based 224 updates. 225 **Naive Alternating.** A simple training procedure that alternates between SFT and RL updates in 226 fixed intervals, without any explicit coordination or shared optimization objective between the two 227 paradigms.

## 253 **5 Related Work**

254 **Reinforcement Learning for Large Reasoning Models.** Recent progress has highlighted the 255 critical role of reinforcement learning in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language 256 models [21, 7]. DeepSeek-R1 introduced a simple yet effective rule-based reward model and 257 demonstrated further gains through multiple rounds of supervised distillation and RL training.

258 LIMR [18] showed that complex reasoning behaviors can emerge from as few as one thousand 259 curated examples from the MATH dataset [11]. 260 In parallel, substantial advances have been made in training recipes for large reasoning models. Chu 261 et al. [4] compare SFT and RL for reasoning tasks and find that RL generalizes significantly better, 262 whereas SFT is prone to overfitting. SimpleRL [31] observes that fine-tuning on short-CoT datasets 263 can harm reasoning ability, while He et al. [9] find that fine-tuning on long-CoT distilled data can 264 improve the reasoning performance of smaller models—especially when used as a warm-up stage 265 before RL training. In practice, two-stage pipelines that combine SFT and RL are commonly used 266 to balance stability and performance. However, existing approaches often rely solely on supervised 267 fine-tuning, which tends to generalize poorly, or on pure RL, which suffers from sample inefficiency 268 and unstable optimization. In this work, we propose the first unified training framework that enables 269 explicit interaction between SFT and RL via a bilevel optimization formulation. This approach 270 offers a new perspective on integrating imitation and exploration for reasoning-centric large language 271 models. 272 **Bilevel Optimization in LLMs.** Bilevel optimization (BLO) is a classical framework for modeling 273 nested learning problems, where an upper-level objective depends on the solution to a lower-level 274 optimization task. Two major classes of methods have been developed to solve BLO problems.

275 Implicit gradient methods [12, 14, 23, 29] compute gradients through the lower-level problem 276 using second-order derivatives. While theoretically robust, these methods are often computationally 277 expensive and memory-prohibitive when applied to large-scale models such as LLMs. In contrast, 278 penalty-based relaxation methods [24, 15, 25, 20] approximate the BLO formulation using only first279 order gradients, making them substantially more scalable and thus better suited for LLM applications. 280 Recent work has explored the use of bilevel optimization in LLMs for tasks such as data selection 281 [19, 26], inverse reinforcement learning [17], and meta-learning [3, 28]. To the best of our knowledge, 282 our work is the first to apply bilevel optimization to reasoning-oriented LLM training, providing a 283 principled approach to integrating supervised and reinforcement learning in a unified framework.

## 284 **6 Conclusion**

285 This work investigates how to effectively integrate supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning 286 to improve the reasoning capabilities of large language models. We begin by analyzing three widely 287 used training paradigms and identify a key limitation of existing multi-stage pipelines: the lack of 288 interaction between SFT and RL. To address this, we propose a simple alternating baseline and further 289 introduce *BRIDGE*, a bilevel optimization framework that models SFT as the upper-level objective 290 and RL as the lower-level objective. By employing a penalty-based relaxation, BRIDGE explicitly 291 encourages joint training to outperform standalone RL, fostering tighter synergy between the two 292 learning paradigms. Empirical results on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that 293 our method consistently outperforms strong baselines in both accuracy and training efficiency. These 294 findings underscore the potential of bilevel optimization as a unifying framework for combining 295 supervised and reward-driven learning in complex reasoning tasks. 296 **Limitations.** While BRIDGE demonstrates promising results, it introduces additional computational 297 overhead due to its nested bilevel optimization structure. Future work includes extending the 298 framework to larger-scale models and more diverse domains such as program synthesis, theorem 299 proving, and scientific reasoning, as well as exploring more efficient optimization strategies to 300 mitigate the computational cost.

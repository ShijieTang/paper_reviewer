## Abstract 19 1 **Introduction**

1 Behavioral Foundation Models (BFMs) proved successful in producing policies for 2 arbitrary tasks in a zero-shot manner, requiring no test-time training or task-specific 3 fine-tuning. Among the most promising BFMs are the ones that estimate the 4 successor measure learned in an unsupervised way from task-agnostic offline data. 5 However, these methods fail to react to changes in the dynamics, making them 6 inefficient under partial observability or when the transition function changes. This 7 hinders the applicability of BFMs in a real-world setting, e.g., in robotics, where the 8 dynamics can unexpectedly change at test time. In this work, we demonstrate that 9 Forward–Backward (FB) representation, one of the methods from the BFM family, 10 cannot distinguish between distinct dynamics, leading to an interference among the 11 latent directions, which parametrize different policies. To address this, we propose 12 a FB model with a transformer-based belief estimator, which greatly facilitates 13 zero-shot adaptation. We also show that partitioning the policy encoding space into 14 dynamics-specific clusters, aligned with the context-embedding directions, yields 15 additional gain in performance. These traits allow our method to respond to the 16 dynamics observed during training and to generalize to unseen ones. Empirically, 17 in the changing dynamics setting, our approach achieves up to a 2x higher zero-shot 18 returns compared to the baselines for both discrete and continuous tasks.

20 One very desirable property of reinforcement learning (RL) agents is their rapid adaptation to new 21 tasks or to environment changes during test-time, without requiring any fine-tuning or planning. 22 Achieving this in as few trials as possible would be even better: the ideal being the zero-shot 23 adaptation [39], where the agent never interacts with the environment at test-time and relies solely 24 on the data it was conditioned with. Behavioral Foundational Models (BFMs) [30, 37] may be 25 considered as a step in this direction, because they can learn a variety of policies from offline data 26 without knowing the rewards. During inference, it is possible to extract a task-specific policy that is 27 optimal or near-optimal in terms of performance [38]. Recent work demonstrates [37] that one of the 28 methods from the BFM family, based on Forward-Backward representation (FB) [38], is especially 29 versatile and can successfully imitate behaviors from unlabeled data. 30 At the same time, FB possesses a fundamental drawback that limits its adaptation ability. In our 31 paper, we show that FB is unable to generalize across different dynamics, such as changes in a 32 transition function (*e.g.*, new obstacles) or an environment with some latent factor variation (e.g.,
33 wind direction). This limitation stems from the way the *successor measure* [8] is estimated: FB
34 averages the future-occupancy state distribution over all observed dynamics, which inevitably causes 35 interference in policy representations. This fact alone may severely constrain the applicability of FB
36 in the real-world scenarios. For example, one of the largest robotics dataset, Open X-Embodiment 37 [7], consists of 22 different robot embodiments, and training FB on each of them simultaneously is 38 infeasible. In Section 3.1, we discuss this limitation and support our claims theoretically.

## 253 4.3 **Do Bfb And Rfb Capture Hidden Properties Of The Environment?**

254 For an agent to refine its policy, it needs to keep track and update the uncertainty over possible 255 environment configurations. Both Belief-FB and Rotation-FB accomplish this. Figure 7 illustrates 256 this phenomenon visually. ## 811 A.2 **Literature**

812 **Domain Adaptation and Transfer Learning in RL.** While our work will focus on domain adapta813 tion applied to estimating successor measure for various dynamics mismatches, we start by briefly 814 reviewing more general ideas in classic domain adaptation and refer to [19] for detailed overview. 815 Most methods for domain adaptation can be categorized into *importance-weighting* [4, 34, 40] and 816 *domain-invariant feature learning* [10, 11, 43, 44] approaches. Former methods estimate the likeli817 hood ratio of examples under samples from target domain versus samples from source, which is then 818 used to recalibrate examples from the source domain. The latter approaches learn a unified repre819 sentation of the environment, targeting to extract only task-relevant abstraction, negating distracting 820 information. 821 The most relevant approach which enables FB representations to generalize across dynamics is Con822 *texual FB* [16]. This approach uses importance-weighting formalism and introduces two classifiers, 823 which estimate the likelihood of transitions (st, at) and (st, at, st+1) being from train or test context 824 and augment the reward function to account for those discrepancies in the dynamics. If augmented 825 reward function lies in the linear span of the Z space during FB training, then the policy can be 826 extracted as described in Equation 4. However, such an approach requires training classifiers from 827 scratch for each novel layout of the environment, limiting its applicability. 828 **Meta-RL.** Another major line of related works, Meta-Reinforcement Learning (Meta-RL), focuses 829 on few-shot domain adaptation to unseen tasks or dynamics [3]. The significant part of research in 830 Meta-RL is dedicated to explicitly learning the *belief* by collecting a history of interactions with the 831 environment on inference during test-time [9, 29, 46]. However, recent works show that it is possible 832 to quantify the *belief* without learning the posterior implicitly [20, 21, 28, 32, 35, 47, 48]. Leveraging 833 in-context ability of transformers [41], one can learn an end-to-end supervised model, while the 834 transformer's context will absorb into robust representation the adaptation-relevant information thus 835 enabling fast adaptation. We also leverage this in-context ability to construct the belief representation 836 of the dynamics the agent currently in, but instead operating in a zero-shot manner.

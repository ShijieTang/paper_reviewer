## Abstract

1 Designing robust reinforcement learning (RL) agents in the presence of imperfect 2 reward signals remains a core challenge. In practice, agents are often trained with 3 proxy rewards that only approximate the true objective, leaving them vulnerable to 4 reward hacking, where high proxy returns arise from unintended or exploitative 5 behaviors. Recent work formalizes this issue using r-correlation between proxy and 6 true rewards, but existing methods like occupancy-regularized policy optimization 7 (ORPO) optimize against a fixed proxy and do not provide strong guarantees against 8 broader classes of correlated proxies. In this work, we formulate reward hacking 9 as a robust policy optimization problem over the space of all r-correlated proxy 10 rewards. We derive a tractable max-min formulation, where the agent maximizes 11 performance under the worst-case proxy consistent with the correlation constraint.

12 We further show that when the reward is a linear function of known features, 13 our approach can be adapted to incorporate this prior knowledge, yielding both 14 improved policies and interpretable worst-case rewards. Experiments across several 15 environments show that our algorithms consistently outperform ORPO in worst16 case returns, and offer improved robustness and stability across different levels 17 of proxy–true reward correlation. These results show that our approach provides 18 both robustness and transparency in settings where reward design is inherently 19 uncertain.

## 20 **1 Introduction**

21 Real-world reinforcement learning (RL) systems often struggle with reward specification: it is 22 notoriously difficult to craft a reward function that perfectly captures the intended goals in all 23 scenarios [1, 2, 3]. In practice, designers rely on proxy rewards that approximate the true objective [4]. 24 However, agents optimizing these imperfect proxies can lead to unintended exploitative behaviors, 25 achieving high proxy returns while yielding poor true outcomes, a phenomenon known as reward 26 hacking [5, 6, 7, 8]. Such reward hacking behaviors are not merely hypothetical; they have led to 27 undesirable or even catastrophic consequences in safety-critical settings (e.g., autonomous driving) [9, 28 10] and are alarmingly common in real-world deployments [11, 12, 13, 14]. Beyond reward hacking, 29 interpretability and transparency of RL policies are increasingly recognized as critical requirements for 30 real-world acceptance [15, 16, 17]. Policymakers and practitioners in safety-critical domains require 31 systems not only to be robust but also interpretable; they must understand which specific decision32 making criteria lead to undesirable outcomes to effectively mitigate risks and ensure compliance with 33 safety regulations [18, 19, 20]. These challenges highlight the need for RL algorithms to address 34 two fundamental challenges: robustness to uncertain or poorly-specified rewards, and interpretability 35 to facilitate oversight and compliance by human stakeholders, especially in high-stakes, real-world 36 environments like traffic control [21], healthcare decision-making [22, 23], and pandemic response 37 strategies [24].

38 Recent work has begun to formalize reward hacking and develop principled mitigations. Laidlaw 39 et al. [25] define a proxy reward to be *r-correlated* with the true reward if it maintains a correlation 40 coefficient r > 0 on state-action pairs encountered by a certain reference (baseline) policy. Notably, 41 their definition permits the proxy and true reward to diverge arbitrarily in parts of the state-action 42 space not visited by the reference policy, precisely the regions an RL agent might exploit under 43 intensive optimization. Using this framework, reward hacking is formalized as the situation in 44 which optimizing an r-correlated proxy yields a policy with lower true reward than that of the 45 reference policy. Building on this definition, Laidlaw et al. propose Occupancy-Regularized Policy 46 Optimization (ORPO) as a mitigation strategy. ORPO augments the standard RL objective with 47 a regularization term that penalizes deviations between the learned policy's occupancy measure 48 (state-action visitation distribution) and that of the reference policy. 49 Despite significant progress, existing solutions to reward hacking show several limitations. First, their 50 effectiveness relies heavily on the choice of the specific proxy reward. However, designing perfect 51 proxies is challenging, and in real-world scenarios, reward proxies are often derived heuristically 52 or empirically from noisy or limited data [26, 27], leading to uncertainty or variability in the exact 53 correlation with true rewards. Therefore, robustness to variations in proxy rewards is crucial for 54 dependable deployment. While the regularization method used by ORPO provides a lower bound on 55 improvement in true reward, its guarantee on the worst-case performance against an adversarially 56 chosen proxy is weak. Second, current methods like ORPO typically treat a reward function as a black 57 box and learn a complex policy with no easily interpretable structure, making it hard to understand 58 why the resulting policy avoids reward hacking or to trust its behavior in novel situations. Further, 59 they cannot be easily adapted to incorporate prior knowledge of the true reward. These shortcomings 60 underscore the need for a more robust and transparent approach to reward hacking in RL. 61 In this work, we formalize reward hacking as a robust RL problem under proxy reward uncertainty and 62 develop new algorithms to address the above gaps. The key idea is to optimize against an adversarial 63 proxy reward rather than trusting a single proxy. We assume the true reward could be any function 64 that remains r-correlated with the proxy (per the reference policy), and we train the agent to perform 65 well against the worst-case such proxy. This approach explicitly accounts for uncertainty in proxy 66 design and guards against unintended exploitative behaviors. Concretely, we propose a max-min 67 formulation in which the policy chooses its strategy to maximize its guaranteed true return while an 68 adversary minimizes the true return by selecting a reward function from the set of all r-correlated 69 proxies. By solving this problem, the agent learns a policy that is robust to all plausible deviations of 70 the proxy reward within the correlation bound. We derive a closed-form solution for the adversary's 71 worst-case reward assignment given any candidate policy, which allows efficient evaluation of the 72 inner minimization and provides insight into how proxy reward flaws are most damaging. Building 73 on this result, we introduce a practical algorithm for Max-Min Policy Optimization that iteratively 74 updates the policy against this worst-case reward signal. 75 Moreover, to improve the tractability and transparency of the inner optimization, we introduce a 76 Linear Max-Min variant of our method. In this variant, we assume the true reward lies in a class of 77 linear functions over known features, allowing us to characterize the worst-case proxy reward as a 78 sparse linear combination of those features. While the policy itself remains parameterized by general 79 neural networks, the learned worst-case reward function becomes interpretable in terms of its feature 80 weights. This provides insight into which aspects of the proxy reward space the policy is robust to or 81 vulnerable against, making it valuable for applications where understanding the failure modes of the 82 reward design is important. 83 Finally, we empirically evaluate the proposed approaches on several challenging environments. 84 Across all domains, our Max-Min and Linear Max-Min policies outperform ORPO in terms of 85 worst-case reward, indicating substantially improved robustness. Moreover, under a large range 86 of proxy-true correlation scenarios, our methods exhibit higher average reward and lower variance 87 compared to ORPO, meaning the performance of our policies remains more consistent and reliable. 88 These findings demonstrate the practical significance of our robust formulation, paving the way for 89 safer and more trustworthy RL deployment in real-world applications. 90 Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: 1) We propose a novel robust RL formulation 91 that explicitly models reward hacking as a max-min optimization problem over proxy rewards 92 constrained by correlation with the true rewards. 2) We develop a practical algorithm for the max-min 93 problem, which is further extended to linear rewards with improved robustness and interpretability. 3) 94 Experiment results demonstrate improved robustness and worst-case rewards across four real-world 95 inspired reward hacking environments.

## 280 **4 Experiment** 281 **4.1 Experiment Setup**

282 We evaluate our method across four realistic benchmark environments: Traffic, Pandemic, *Glucose* 283 *Monitoring*, and *Tomato Watering GridWorld*. These environments were originally proposed in [36, 5] 284 and represent diverse forms of proxy reward hacking, including misweighting, ontological mismatch, 285 and scope misalignment [36]. Each setting presents unique challenges in reward specification and 286 policy robustness. A detailed description of the environments and their respective reward structures is 287 provided in Appendix E.4. In each of the four environments, we train policies using both our Max-Min 288 and Linear Max-Min optimization algorithms. For baselines, we compare against the ORPO policy. 289 To isolate the impact of discriminator training, we also include an ablation: ORPO*, where we train the 290 ORPO policy using the same full discriminator training schedule as in our algorithms. In contrast, the performance of Linear Max-Min declines in this case, 320 suggesting its advantage diminishes when prior assumptions about feature structure are inaccurate. 321 We find that ORPO* exhibits better worst-case performance than the original ORPO. Notably, in the Pandemic environment, we observe no 325 such unvisited state-action pairs, and the discriminator outputs remain small across all policies. This 326 could be due to either the discriminator network not being fully optimized or its inability to capture rare events that fall outside the support of πref 327 . Developing more reliable techniques for handling 328 such rare or unseen state-action pairs remains an open direction for future work.

329 We also report the adversarial weight vectors θ for each policy. These weights reveal which features 330 are most vulnerable to proxy exploitation under the learned policy and can be used to diagnose and 331 revise the proxy reward function, thereby improving robustness. This highlights the interpretability 332 benefits of our framework. Moreover, several patterns emerge from the results. In the Traffic 333 environment, first, we observe a clear dominance of the headway feature, with all methods assigning 334 it the highest weight. This suggests that headway is the most critical component exposed to reward 335 hacking under correlation constraints. Second, the acceleration feature is consistently downweighted 336 across all methods. This occurs because the expected 342 feature value under their policies is exactly zero, making the correlation constraint inactive for that 343 dimension. Interestingly, this feature plays a significant role in the adversarial rewards for both 344 ORPO and Linear Max-Min, with their corresponding θ assigning non-negligible weight to it (e.g., 345 0.95 and 0.97 respectively). ## 373 **5 Conclusion**

374 In this work, we propose a robust policy optimization framework that explicitly accounts for reward 375 hacking by training policies against the worst-case proxy reward drawn from a correlation-constrained 376 uncertainty set. Our approach formalizes reward hacking as a robust optimization problem and 377 introduces both a Max-Min formulation with a closed-form adversarial reward and a Linear Max-Min 378 variant that further improves interpretability and tractability. We develop efficient algorithms and 379 empirically validate our methods across diverse environments known to exhibit reward hacking 380 behavior. Our results demonstrate that both Max-Min and Linear Max-Min policies achieve stronger 381 worst-case performance and improved stability compared to prior baselines such as ORPO.

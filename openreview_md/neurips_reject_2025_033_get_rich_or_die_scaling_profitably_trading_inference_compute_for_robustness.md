## Abstract

1 Recent work shows that increasing inference-time compute through generation of 2 long reasoning traces improves not just capability scores, but robustness to various 3 text jailbreaks designed to control models or lower their guardrails. However, 4 multimodal reasoning offers comparatively little defense against vision jailbreaks, 5 which typically succeed by creating noise-like perturbations. When attacking a 6 robust model, vision attacks are also capable of and often must resort to producing 7 human-interpretable perturbations. Rather than operating in a model's blind-spot 8 or out of its training distribution, such interpretable attacks construct familiar 9 concepts connected to the attacker's goal. Inspired by the ability of robust models 10 to force attacks into this space that appears more in-distribution for reasoning tasks, 11 we posit the Robustness from Inference Compute Hypothesis (RICH): defending 12 against attacks with inference compute (like reasoning) profits as those attacks 13 become more in-distribution. To test this, we adversarially attack models of varying 14 robustness with black-box-transfer and white-box attacks. RICH predicts a rich15 get-richer dynamic: models that start with higher initial robustness gain more 16 robustness benefits from increases in inference-time compute. Consistent with 17 RICH, we find that robust models benefit more from increased compute, whereas 18 non-robust models show little to no improvement. Our work suggests that inference19 time compute can be an effective defense against adversarial attacks, provided 20 the base model has some degree of robustness. In particular, layering disparate 21 train-time and test-time defenses aids robustness not additively, but synergistically.

## 22 **1 Introduction**

23 Foundation models have grown increasingly capable with the scaling of their pretraining and post24 training [Kaplan et al., 2020, Hoffmann et al., 2022, Sardana and Frankle, 2023]. More recently, 25 inference-time compute scaling to produce long reasoning trajectories has proved capable of generat26 ing human-expert-level performances on various benchmarks [OpenAI et al., 2024, OpenAI, 2025, 27 Guo et al., 2025, DeepMind, 2025, Anthropic, 2025]. However, despite these advances, adversarial 28 robustness remains an open challenge, particularly in safety-critical applications such as autonomous 29 driving. Neural networks are known to be vulnerable to carefully designed inputs that can subvert 30 their intended behavior, bypass guardrails, or generate harmful output [Szegedy et al., 2013]. Solving 31 this challenge is the key to a successful deployment of AI in real-world applications. 32 Recent work by Zaremba et al. [2025] represents an exciting direction, showing that inference-time 33 compute offers an intriguing dual benefit: not only does it improve task performance, but it also 34 enhances robustness to text jailbreaks. However, we found that this benefit does not extend cleanly to 35 the vision domain (see Figure 2). Multimodal reasoning [Liu et al., 2023, Zaremba et al., 2025], while 36 effective at tasks like visual question answering, offers comparatively little defense against vision 37 jailbreaks, which typically succeed by introducing noise-like perturbations that remain uninterpretable 38 to both humans and models. These perturbations frequently occur in underexplored or off-distribution 39 regions of the input space, where noise-like distortions mislead the model or confuse its semantic 40 understanding of the image, making additional computation at test time only marginally effective. 41 A separate line of work on adversarially trained image classification models and vision-language mod42 els has shown that increasing robustness of the model alters the nature of adversarial attacks: rather 43 than remaining imperceptible or noise-like, attacks become visually interpretable and often resemble 44 semantically meaningful concepts (e.g., textures, patterns, or objects aligned with the attacker's 45 objective) [Gaziv et al., 2023, Bartoldson et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025, Fort and Lakshminarayanan, 46 2024]. Appearing as everyday objects, these interpretable perturbations may be closer to the model's 47 training distribution, and we suspect they may thus be more amenable to reasoning-based defenses - 48 particularly those implemented by increased inference-time compute. 49 Inspired by this observation, we introduce the Robustness from Inference Compute Hypothesis 50 (RICH): inference-time compute (e.g., long reasoning traces) is most effective as a defense when 51 attacks are forced into in-distribution regimes understandable by the model. In other words, inference52 compute-based defenses work best when the model is already somewhat robust, and thus able to push 53 attackers into a domain where test-time reasoning is effective. 54 RICH predicts a "rich-get-richer" dynamic: models that begin with higher baseline robustness gain 55 disproportionately more robustness benefits from additional inference-time compute. In contrast, 56 non-robust models, which remain vulnerable to out-of-distribution (OOD) perturbations, see compute 57 scaling provide little to no defense against attacks that easily generate data that is OOD for the model. 58 To test this hypothesis, we conduct adversarial evaluations of VLMs with varying degrees of robust59 ness using both white-box and black-box-transfer attacks. We systematically vary inference-time 60 compute and analyze how its defense benefits scale as a function of base robustness. As shown in 61 Figure 1, more robust models exhibit increased resistance as compute scales, with attacks requiring 62 more steps or exhibiting reduced success rates. Conversely, non-robust models are comparatively 63 brittle regardless of the amount of reasoning at test time. 64 These findings demonstrate that inference-time compute and train-time defenses interact not additively 65 but synergistically: together they provide greater robustness than either alone. The contributions of 66 this work are as follows:
67 1. We propose a hypothesis that explains prior failures of inference-time compute to signifi68 cantly boost robustness to vision attacks, and which suggests that these failures could be 69 addressed by using more robust base models. 70 2. We test our hypothesis using attacks from prior work and novel attacks. Our novel white-box 71 vision attack is the first white-box attack used to test the multimodal robustness benefits of 72 scaling inference-time compute, to the best of our knowledge. 73 3. Consistent with our hypothesis, we demonstrate that inference-time compute provides larger 74 benefits when the base model is more robust. This result clarifies how to improve robustness 75 in exchange for inference-time compute, with a better rate of return.

## 76 **2 Background And Exploratory Findings**

77 Zaremba et al. [2025] found that scaling inference-time compute defends against adversarial attacks, 78 driving attack success rates towards zero for many settings. However, this inference-time scaling 79 seems to fall short for vision attacks defended against via multimodal reasoning. Indeed, as shown in 80 Figure 2, the accuracy of o1-v on clean images - i.e., data that isn't attacked - in a low-compute 81 setting (left panel) is not able to be reached on attacked images (right panel), even when using the 82 highest level of inference-time compute.

83 Given the high economic cost of raising inference-time compute to such levels, this o1-v result of 84 Figure 2 suggests that inference-time compute may be a prohibitively expensive defense strategy. 85 Indeed, these images are affected only by static black-box attacks optimized for a separate model 86 [Dong et al., 2023] - white-box vision attacks on o1-v itself would be much more difficult to defend 87 against and could pose an insurmountable financial burden if addressed via reasoning. Moreover, 88 Zaremba et al. [2025] leaves unclear whether reasoning can even defend against white-box vision 89 attacks (studying only black-box vision attacks) and notes that enhancing robustness to vision 90 adversarial attacks remains an important area for future research. 91 In this paper, we aim to clarify whether inference-time compute scaling can be a cost-effective 92 defense to attacks of various strengths, and (further) how such scaling might be improved. Our 93 experiments focus on vision attacks and thus multimodal reasoning. Our initial testing in Figure 2 94 shows that Delta2LLaVA-v1.5 Wang et al. [2025] - a highly adversarially robust model (RM) - does 95 not require any inference-time compute scaling to outperform the robustness of o1-v at its highest 96 inference-time compute level (see Section 3.2 for experiment details). This further calls into question 97 whether inference-compute scaling as a defense is worth its price. 98 Interestingly, we also see that non-robust models (LLaVA-v1.5 in Figure 2) may fail to benefit from 99 scaling of inference-compute on attacked data, even when they receive benefits on clean data. This 100 negative result may not be surprising, as attacks may leverage a model's inability to operate correctly 101 on data outside its training distribution, and it is not clear that adding more reasoning would be 102 able to facilitate the removal of such an attack's effects. In other words, while the noise-like pattern 103 such attacks have may be negligible to a human - see Figure 3 for an example of an adversarial 104 Attack-Bard image that humans can understand - it could nonetheless represent a significant shift 105 away from the training distribution of the model. Corroborating this, Figure 3 shows that LLaVA-v1.5 106 produces an image description that is completely unrelated to the target ("American Coot"), which 107 will prevent reasoning from providing a benefit regardless of the inference-time compute level. 108 Finally, it is necessary to consider the potential ability of inference-time compute scaling as a defense 109 on a class of vision adversarial attacks that recent literature has highlighted for its effectiveness against 110 models with state-of-the-art robustness. These attacks do not appear as noisy versions of their base 111 images; i.e., they depart from the pattern in Figure 3. Prior work shows that such adversarial images are produced when attacking sufficiently robust 115 networks: intuitively, if a robustified model cannot be attacked through subtle perturbations, then 116 visually instantiating the attacker's target can become a less-difficult path towards attack success. This attack setup is analogous to real world settings, which 122 include following safety specifications regardless of adversarially persuasive user inputs [Zaremba 123 et al., 2025], and self-driving of vehicles using multimodal systems that must understand the need 124 to prioritize specific modalities (e.g., a human driver's command to stop should override a model's 125 potentially hijacked understanding of the visual scene). 127 Critical to our work, the aforementioned interpretable-attack strategy suggests that, when adversar128 ially attacking robust models, the attacked data may not go outside (or as far outside) the training 129 distribution as it does when attacking less-robust models. Instead, attacks on robust models may rely 130 on their ability to produce responses based on an accurate understanding of the attacked data. If this 131 is the case, then reasoning may become more relevant as a defense. In other words, robust models 132 may ease the problem of applying safety specifications to attacked inputs [Zaremba et al., 2025] by 133 making reasoning about the relationship of those inputs to the specifications easier. In sum, robust 134 models may reduce the problem of providing a robust response to one that is solvable by reasoning of 135 the type that the model was trained to do, whereas it's unclear that reasoning on out-of-distribution 136 representations from non-robust models has a chance to succeed. 137 These exploratory findings and subsequent analyses motivate the following hypothesis, which we 138 validate via rigorous testing in the remainder of this work.

The Robustness from Inference Compute Hypothesis. *Inference-time compute is most effective as* a defense when attacks are forced into in-distribution regimes understandable by the model. 139

## 140 **3 Methodology** 158 **3.1 White-Box Pgd Attack**

141 We study how inference-time compute provides robustness to adversarial visual jailbreaks by testing 142 VLMs with varying robustness levels (low, medium, and high): LLaVA-v1.5 [Liu et al., 2024], FARE- 143 LLaVA-v1.5 [Schlarmann et al., 2024], and Delta2LLaVA-v1.5 [Wang et al., 2025]. While Zaremba 144 et al. [2025] consider a non-robust reasoning model, our approach makes explicit the potential effect 145 of robust vision representations, or the lack thereof, on measuring the benefits of reasoning defenses. 146 We adopt LLaVA-v1.5 as our baseline VLM. While this model operates with a strong connection 147 between the visual and text domains, due to its visual-instruction tuning, it is not robust to adver148 sarial image attacks as neither its image encoder nor its language model experienced adversarial 149 training. Contrast this with FARE-LLaVA-v1.5 which replaces the frozen CLIP image encoder 150 with a robust version achieved through unsupervised adversarial finetuning on ImageNet. Finally, 151 Delta2LLaVA-v1.5 adds two levels of defense: full, web-scale adversarial contrastive CLIP pretrain152 ing and adversarial visual instruction tuning. Increased adversarial training yields strong benefits 153 to performance. For example,Wang et al. [2025] report that when comparing LLaVAs on a task 154 requiring visual reasoning like VQAv2 [Goyal et al., 2017], Delta2LLaVA-v1.5 achieves 59.5%
155 accuracy under a ℓ∞ ε = 4/255 attack while FARE-LLaVA-v1.5 reaches 31% and non-robust 156 LLaVA-v1.5 obtains 0%. For our FARE-LLaVA-v1.5 experiments, we use the FARE-CLIP encoder 157 finetuned with ε = 2/255 under the ℓ∞ norm.

159 We evaluate VLM robustness under a novel white-box adversarial attack. Our attack creates a conflict 160 between modalities by providing correct information in the text input (e.g., mentioning that a soccer 161 ball is "round" as shown in Figure 4) while simultaneously applying a PGD attack on the image input 162 that targets an incorrect model output (e.g., "Cube"). 163 **Inference-time Compute Scaling** To investigate how additional inference-time compute affects 164 robustness, we use textual repetition to raise computational effort. Specifically, we repeat the correct 165 text description K times in the instruction prompt, and we explicitly instruct the model to defer to 166 the text modality when the text and vision inputs conflict. Higher K represents increased levels of 167 inference-time compute. Notably, this is not the same inference-time compute scaling performed by 168 reasoning models like o1, but it allows us to investigate how naively scaling inference-time compute 169 affects robustness. While our black-box experiments provide closer proxies for prior work with closed 170 models on scaling reasoning for robustness [Zaremba et al., 2025] - see Section 4.4 - we expect 171 our novel white-box methodology to provide a strong test of inference-time compute's robustness 172 benefits. In particular, scaling K may make the model more inclined to defer to the answer given in 173 the text input; i.e., the probability of the model calling a ball "red" is expected to increase with the 174 number of in-context statements describing the ball with this color, consistent with patterns found in 175 the model's training data. This increased evidence for choosing a particular value through scaling K 176 can be seen as proxying for the ability of state-of-the-art reasoning systems to produce increasing 177 amounts of evidence for choosing a particular value through a reasoning trace. 178 **Attack Details** For each attack instance, we run a PGD attack with step size 0.1 for 100 iterations, 179 using a perturbation budget ε ∈ {16/255, 64/255}. At each step, we track both the cross-entropy 180 loss of the target tokens and whether the model generates the target response. We record the minimum 181 number of PGD steps required for successful attack (lower values indicate lower robustness). The 182 attack is considered failed if the model does not generate the target response after all 100 steps. 183 Experiments were conducted using a single NVIDIA 80GB H100 GPU.

## 184 **3.2 Black-Box Transfer Attacks**

203 **Attack-Bard with Chain of Thought** Additionally, we leverage Attack-Bard to examine black-box 204 attack success when the VLM's intrinsic reasoning capabilities are invoked through chain of Thought 205 (CoT) prompting techniques [Wei et al., 2022, Kojima et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022]. This setup 206 does not use an external judge and instead asks the model to classify the image with varying degrees 207 of intermediate reasoning. For each image, we construct a multiple choice question including the 208 true label and 29 other answers chosen from the label set at random. We devise a low inference-time 209 compute, no CoT, setting where the model is prompted to select the correct label from the provided 210 choices. In the high inference compute regime, we apply CoT reasoning to elicit classification from 211 step-by-step thinking. Image labels were generated from the VLM using greedy sampling with 0 212 temperature generating a maximum of 5 and 500 tokens for the low and high respective settings.

213 Details on the CoT prompts can be found in B.1. Experiments were conducted using a single 80GB
214 Nvidia H100 GPU. 185 We also test RICH on a dataset of transferred, black-box adversarial examples using an image 186 classification task. Attack-Bard consists of 200 images generated from a white-box adversarial attack 187 on an ensemble of surrogate models [Dong et al., 2023]. These images were optimized for transfer 188 to Bard and GPT-4V with ε = 16/255 under the ℓ∞ norm. The clean counterparts to these 200 189 images are used to measure the baseline strength of each model's visual perception and the benefits 190 of adaptive inference-time compute on classifying natural images. 191 **Attack-Bard with Augmented Reasoning** We evaluate each VLM for its classification accuracy 192 on Attack-Bard, under low and high inference-time compute settings. We apply each model to predict 193 the class label of an input image using its multimodal context —the image pixels and the instruction 194 prompt. As the VLMs surveyed have moderate instruction-following capabilities and struggle on 195 their own to classify an image when prompted with the full label set, we augment each VLM with 196 adaptive inference-time compute and predict the label in two stages. First, we prompt the VLM to 197 provide a description for each image. Then using this description, we apply Claude 3.7 Sonnet to 198 judge which label best matches the generated description [Anthropic, 2025]. Using the "extended 199 thinking" feature of the judge, we create low and high inference-time compute settings. Both the low 200 and high inference-time compute settings use a temperature of 1 and set the max number of tokens 201 generated to 20,000. The high inference-time compute setting uses a budget of 16,000 thinking 202 tokens. Details on the Claude prompts used can be found in Appendix B.1.

## 215 **4 Experiments** 216 **4.1 Does Inference-Compute Scaling Help All Models Equally?**

217 As Zaremba et al. [2025] only studied one model, it's unclear if scaling inference-compute provides 218 the same benefits regardless of the base model (and its robustness). E.g., a constant benefit might 219 be expected if reasoning aids defense by making attack optimization more complex. Alternatively, 220 RICH suggests that reasoning's robustness benefits depend on the base model's robustness. 221 To test this, we use white-box PGD attacks on models with increasing levels of adversarial robustness. 222 If RICH is correct, we would expect to see robust models are harder to attack at a given inference223 time compute level, relative to less robust models. Alternatively, if the benefits of scaling inference 224 compute are unrelated to the model, we would expect that there's no relationship between a base 225 model's robustness and the benefits it obtains from scaling inference compute. 226 Figure 5 shows the PGD attack loss curves for VLMs with increasing inference-compute levels 227 when ε = 64/255. It is found that the loss for the most robust model (Delta2LLaVA-v1.5) has a 228 substantial rise when the compute level rises, leading to substantially increased numbers of PGD 229 steps to break the model. In contrast, models with lower robustness do not exhibit such changes. This 230 observation is consistent with RICH. Specifically, the benefits of scaling inference compute depend 231 on the robustness of the model.

Does Inference-Compute Scaling Help All Models Equally? No, we find that inferencecompute scaling benefits robustness more when the model is initially more robust.

## 233 **4.2 Can Inference-Compute Scaling Only Benefit Robustness In Select Models?**

234 We have seen that the benefits of scaling inference-time compute depend on the model. However, it 235 remains unclear why this is the case. One possibility is that only Delta2-LLaVA-1.5 benefits notably 236 because it was visually instruction tuned while under adversarial attacks [Wang et al., 2025]. Indeed, 237 FARE had comparatively light adversarial training that only fine-tuned the vision embedding model 238 [Schlarmann et al., 2024]. Thus, we may expect that only Delta2-LLaVA-1.5 can significantly benefit 239 from inference-time compute scaling in our setup because it was the only model trained to perform 240 multimodal reasoning when under attack.

241 Alternatively, reasoning may be able to support robustness as long as the data being reasoned 242 about is close enough to being in-distribution. We might expect this to be the case if, for example, 243 inference-time compute scaling boosts defenses by enhancing the model's ability to perform correct 244 classification given an accurate representation of the image, and if less robust models are capable of 245 providing accurate representations of attacked images as long as the perturbation is small enough.

246 To test this, we used a smaller perturbation budget ε = 16/255, bringing the attacked images closer 247 to the distribution the model was trained on. ## 294 **5 Discussion**

295 Scaling inference-time compute has been shown to provide many benefits that even extend to increased 296 robustness. Enhancing robustness and other model safety/security capabilities is key to obtaining 297 the trust needed for widespread use and benefits of frontier AI. Prior work found that this robustness 298 benefit of increasing inference-time compute was limited when adversaries used vision attacks. We 299 proposed a hypothesis to explain this limitation as well as how to ensure robustness benefits from 300 inference-time compute scaling in cost-effective manner. Our hypothesis, the Robustness from 301 Inference Compute Hypothesis, was validated through a variety of experiments that include novel 302 white-box and previously explored black-box attacks. 303 In Appendix A, we discuss additional related work on out of distribution (OOD) robustness, adversar304 ial attacks, and adversarial training.

305 **Limitations** We explored a phenomenon first uncovered in a large-scale reasoning model (o1)
306 using experiments at a comparatively much smaller scale. While our model scale facilitates tests of 307 the most adversarially robust VLMs that we know of [Wang et al., 2025], it is necessary to validate 308 our findings at larger scales, which see widespread deployment of models and which pose the largest 309 potential harm when attacks are successful. Towards this, future work could adversarially train larger 310 (possibly frontier-scale) models to test our core hypothesis more broadly. 311 **Broader Impact** As LLM capabilities improve, studying defenses against adversarial attacks that 312 lower their safety guardrails can potentially enhance trust in and benefits of LLM deployment in vari313 ous settings. However, automated defenses like scaling inference compute should be complemented 314 by attentive and responsible evaluation/monitoring.

## 434 **A Additional Related Work**

435 Model performance degrades when data is adversarially perturbed by attacks that humans are robust 436 to [Szegedy et al., 2013]. Adversarial training [Goodfellow et al., 2014, Madry et al., 2017] can help 437 improve model robustness. However, according to RobustBench [Croce et al., 2020], the robustness 438 problem is still unsolved on toy datasets like CIFAR-10. Recent work predicts that an alternative 439 paradigm is needed, as scaling adversarial training may be a computationally infeasible solution 440 [Bartoldson et al., 2024]. 441 Additionally, Bartoldson et al. [2024] showed that attacking a robustified network by altering an 442 image of a horse would lead to the labeling of the image as a dog by the robustified network and 443 *by humans*; i.e., the image was altered to resemble a dog rather than affected by noise-like patterns 444 that humans can ignore. Additional corroboration in Fort [2025] is shown for a model that was not 445 directly adversarially trained, suggesting that robustness itself rather than a means of achieving it 446 might be directly linked to interpretability. 447 We show that this phenomenon plays a major role in how inference-time attacks and defenses play 448 out for multimodal reasoning models. Specifically, we find that PGD attacks [Madry et al., 2017] 449 on VLMs like LLaVA 1.5 [Liu et al., 2024] proceed by generating noise-like patterns and quickly 450 achieve their targeted output [Bailey et al., 2023]. However, attacks on robustified VLMs like 451 Delta2LLaVA-v1.5 [Wang et al., 2025] produce semantically interpretable features and struggle to 452 successfully achieve the targeted model output. An example of this is shown in Figure 4. 453 This suggests that, while adversarial attacks are typically seen as producing out-of-distribution (OOD) 454 data, this is not necessarily the case (especially in robust models). Our core hypothesis (RICH) 455 suggests that reasoning can provide larger robustness benefits when model attacks are not OOD. 456 In this work, we explored the concept of adversarially robust models' ability to keep adversarial 457 attacks in distribution, but our hypothesis may relate to other ways in which OOD can arise. However, 458 while OOD robustness can be enhanced by scaling Fort et al. [2021], even the most robust near-OOD 459 detectors are still brittle to targeted adversarial attacks [Fort, 2022], accenting that robustness against 460 distribution shifts and robustness against active attacks might be different kinds of phenomena. 461 Importantly, Howe et al. [2024] found that larger LLMs tend to be more robust, despite not being 462 adversarially trained. Thus, it's possible that the relatively strong defensive benefit of scaling 463 inference-time compute on text input attacks is consistent with the Robustness from Inference 464 Compute Hypothesis. Specifically, inference-time compute may have benefitted LLM robustness in 465 Zaremba et al. [2025] because the frontier-scale model being tested was already somewhat robust to 466 the text attacks considered, even though that model had no explicit training to encourage robustness 467 to attacks.

## Abstract

1 Collective action against algorithmic systems, which enables groups to promote 2 their own interests, is poised to grow. Hence, there will be growth in the size and 3 the number of distinct collectives. Currently, there is no formal analysis of how 4 coordination challenges within a collective can impact downstream outcomes, or 5 how multiple collectives may affect each other's success. In this work, we aim to 6 provide guarantees on the success of collective action in the presence of both co7 ordination noise and multiple groups. Our insight is that data generated by either 8 multiple collectives or by coordination noise can be viewed as originating from 9 multiple data distributions. Using this framing, we derive bounds on the success 10 of collective action. We conduct experiments to study the effects of noise on col11 lective action. We find that sufficiently high levels of noise can reduce the success 12 of collective action. In certain scenarios, large noise can sink a collective suc13 cess rate from 100% to just under 60%. We identify potential trade-offs between 14 collective size and coordination noise; for example, a collective that is twice as 15 big but with four times more noise experiencing worse outcomes than the smaller, 16 more coordinated one. This work highlights the importance of understanding nu17 anced dynamics of strategic behavior in algorithmic systems.

## 18 **1 Introduction**

19 As large platforms (social media, e-commerce) grow, groups within them find it increasingly im20 portant and necessary to act strategically (e.g., by changing ratings) in these platforms to get their 21 desired outcome. From delivery drivers coordinating to earn higher wages [SHMD24], to fans promoting their favorite artist [XZF+ 22 25, Nug21], to protesting controversial businesses [Pay24], this 23 type of strategic behavior is poised to grow. Strategic behavior ultimately affects the downstream 24 data distributions used to train models. As the incentive to participate increases, we need a more 25 nuanced understanding of the impacts on a system; this includes both understanding what happens 26 with multiple distinct collectives acting on a system as well as considering how internal collective 27 dynamics may impact outcomes. She finds that a clear organizational structure and well-defined roles are crucial—one 33 possible interpretation is that smaller groups can more readily and faithfully implement and coor34 dinate their actions. An analogy in the computational world is "noise": unwanted modifications 35 or deviations from intended behavior (e.g., signal processing). This "noise" can result from poor 36 coordination and may reduce effectiveness.

37 In algorithmic settings, collective action is mediated through strategic data modifications to influence 38 model behavior. These changes in data distribution can have significant downstream ramifications. 39 [HMMDZ23] provided theoretical bounds on the success of collective action in the case of a sin40 gle, unified collective. The collective modifies their data (e.g., by inserting a watermark), which is 41 then used in training. The learning algorithm observes a distribution P: a mixture of data induced 42 by the collective's action (P1) and the underlying data distribution P0. These bounds help iden43 tify the conditions under which collective action may be more successful. However, this analysis 44 assumes perfect coordination within the collective. [KVKS25] developed a framework to identify 45 the factors affecting collective action involving multiple collectives, using simulations to illustrate 46 several outcomes. However, they do not provide any theoretical bounds on success based on the data 47 distributions induced by each collective. A formal understanding of how both noise and multiple 48 collectives interact is key to understanding real-world algorithmic collective action. 49 In this work, we provide new guarantees on algorithmic collective action in both the presence of 50 noise and multiple distinct collectives. We do this by introducing *multiple distributions* that feed the 51 ultimate observed data distribution P. Instead of considering just a mixture of P0 and P1, we study 52 how the addition of P2 affects a collective's success. Importantly, the source of these distributions 53 can vary: it could occur from multiple distinct collectives or because of coordination challenges 54 within a single collective inducing a new, second data distribution.

55 A collective that is highly coordinated is one where the size of P2 is small and similar to P1, while 56 ones with less cohesion have a larger gap between P1 and P2. Multiple collectives can be analyzed 57 as behavior coming from two distinct distributions. Our contributions are as follows: 58 **Theoretical bounds with multiple distributions:** We are the first to establish the lower bounds for 59 the success of collective action in the presence of multiple distributions. Prior work has either only 60 analyzed a single distribution coming from a single collective or provided empirical outcomes with 61 multiple collectives [KVKS25, HMMDZ23]. We are able to relate this bound to the similarity of 62 the distributions and collective size. This approach can effectively handle many different scenarios: 63 including when there's a fraction of a collective performing actions imperfectly or when multiple 64 distinct collectives are acting upon a system. 65 **Empirical impact of noise:** We study the impact of noise in collective action. Prior work 66 [HMMDZ23] has only shown outcomes with perfectly coordinated collectives. We find that differ67 ent types of noise can impact the success of collective action and find in some situations, smaller, 68 less noisy groups perform better than larger groups with more noise. 69 We first present a formulation of the algorithmic collective action problem. We establish bounds on 70 the success of collective action with multiple distributions. Our experiments extend [HMMDZ23] 71 by considering a text classification task with noise. We find scenarios where noise sinks success a 72 task from nearly 100% without noise to under 60%. We discuss possible trade-offs in size and noise 73 where small groups with less noise (0.25% collective size with 10% noise) outperform larger ones 74 with more noise (0.5% with 40% noise), which can inform organizers of collective action.

Different types of collectives and algorithmic systems might exhibit trade-offs between size 273 and minimizing noise (if such a trade-off exists). For organizers of collective action knowledge of 274 these characteristics can help to decide whether resources should be allocated to expand the total 275 collective size or to more tightly coordinate within a small group. 277 Algorithmic collective action can generally be used when there's a difference between the goals of 278 those who generate data and are affected by models and those who train and deploy them. Our 279 aim is to continue the work on promoting socially valuable use cases of collective action ([FS22, AHJ+ 280 22]); however, we recognize that there could be malicious use cases. We believe that model 281 developers should be aware of the strategic behavior that can lead to different data distributions. 283 We assumed that we could easily segregate between distributions. In some cases, this may be more 284 natural (multiple collectives) while in others (benign noise) may be difficult to do in practice. We 285 also considered a limited set of "noise" variations on a small set of data. Future work could explore 286 more ecologically motivated types of deviations, including non-independent noise and adversarial 287 actions. We also only considered a small classification task, different types of learning paradigms 288 may be affected by noise differently - this would be an important avenue for future work. 290 We investigated the role of multiple distributions on the success of collective action. We derived 291 lower bounds on the success rate in relation to the cross-signal overlap and suboptimality gap. We 292 empirically evaluated noise in collective action. We found that noise variation matters, and that 293 noise that affects labels more severally impacts collective action. We note that, for organizers, un294 derstanding the trade-offs between group size and the potential noise is important; different systems 295 and types of actions may push organizers to invest more in effective coordination in a small group 296 vs expansion. As strategic interest on algorithmic systems grows, both developers of algorithms 297 and organizers of collective action must be aware of the potential that differing distributions has on 298 system outcomes.

## 394 **B Experimental Details**

395 As described in the main body we finetune a distilbert-based-uncased [SDCW19] for five epochs with default hyperparameters using Hugging Face transformer library [WDS+ 396 20]. This 397 experimental setting is the same as [HMMDZ23, KVKS25]. The resume dataset from [JT21] was 398 split into 20,000 training points and 5,000 test points. The default or baseline strategy was to place 399 a specific character (in this case the '{' character) every 20 words. We evaluated on targeting the 400 'Software Developer' class, except for the 'low frequency class' where the target was 'Database 401 Administrator.' 402 During training, each training point is select with a probability r (the noise fraction) to be affected 403 by a specific noise condition. If the point was selected to be noised, the noise would be applied to 404 the text, and, for certain contains, the label as well. 405 **Random-Subset:** Instead of the intended character, a character from the list [U+2E18, '!', '?', '·'] 406 was selected uniformly at random to be used. 407 **Random-Full:** Same as above, but the character chosen was from the set of all single characters in 408 the vocabulary of the tokenizer. 409 **Displaced-Original:** Keep the intended character, but instead of placing every 20 words, choose 410 uniformly at random (5-30 instances) places to insert the character. 411 **Displaced-Full:** Combine the character selection of Random-Full with the placement behavior of 412 Displaced-Original. 413 To evaluate whether the signal was planted successfully, for each data point in the test set, we applied 414 the "true" signal and evaluated whether the trained classifier would predict the target class. We used 415 the top-one accuracy as done in [HMMDZ23] as the metric of success. 416 All experimental conditions were run 15 times. Experiments were run on a ppc64le based cluster 417 with V100 Nvidia GPUs. Each iteration took 30 − 40 minutes to complete.

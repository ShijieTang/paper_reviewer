## 16 **1 Introduction**

17 Sampling from densities of the form 18 with ω evaluable pointwise but Z intractable, is a central problem in machine learning [21, 30] 19 and statistics [3, 31], and has applications in scientific fields like physics [1, 33, 53], chemistry 20 [17, 23, 24], and many other fields involving probabilistic models. 21 Many established sampling algorithms are inherently iterative, with the accuracy of the final sam22 ples depending heavily on the number of steps. Classical Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) 23 methods asymptotically converge to the target distribution with an infinite number of steps [27, 39]. 24 Nonetheless, the finite number of feasible steps in real-world applications means that MCMC can 25 only provide an approximation and cannot guarantee an exact solution. As an improvement, more 26 recent diffusion-samplers [7, 50, 55] guarantee convergence in a finite number of steps. However, they 27 often necessitate hundreds of iterations to yield high-quality samples. Such iterative samplers tend to 28 suffer from slow mixing, making them still impractical for use in large models and resource-limited 29 scenarios. 30 From another perspective, recent work on diffusion generative models [22, 44, 47, 48] has demon31 strated the feasibility of fewer-step sampling. This can be achieved using knowledge distillation 32 [41, 46] or consistency training [46], potentially enabling even single-step generation. However, 33 directly applying the distillation techniques to distillate existing diffusion samplers is challenging, 34 as it often requires large datasets of samples that are expensive to collect in practice. Moreover, the 35 learning targets of diffusion samplers and consistency training are conflicted in some aspects. These 36 make reducing the sampling steps of diffusion samplers a challenging problem.

37 In this paper, we propose *consistent diffusion samplers* to produce high-quality samples in a single 38 step. We first show that diffusion-based samplers can be *consistently distilled* into single-step diffusion 39 samplers and propose the Consistency-Distilled Diffusion Samplers (CDDS) approach. Instead of 40 storing a large dataset of fully diffused samples, CDDS exploits incomplete trajectories and noisy 41 samples encountered during the diffusion process, hence reducing the unnecessary costs. We further 42 introduce the Self-Consistent Diffusion Sampler (SCDS) method that does not require a pretrained 43 diffusion sampler. Instead, it fully amortizes exploration by jointly learning both diffusion sampling 44 and large cut off steps that match the outcome of paths of small steps. This enables single-step 45 sampling yet retains the option to refine samples through multiple iterations if desired, subsuming 46 existing diffusion-based approaches. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 47 - We show that diffusion-based samplers for unnormalized distributions can be effectively distilled 48 into single-step consistent samplers without pre-collecting large datasets of samples. 49 - We introduce a self-consistent diffusion sampler that learns to perform single-step sampling by 50 jointly training diffusion-based transitions and large shortcut steps via a self-consistency criterion. 51 This method only trains one neural network and does not require pretrained samplers or high-quality 52 datasets. 53 - Through extensive evaluations on synthetic and real unnormalized distributions, we demonstrate 54 that our method delivers competitive sample quality while drastically reducing sampling steps.

## 55 **2 Related Work**

56 **Monte Carlo-based Samplers.** Monte Carlo-based Samplers, such as Markov chain Monte Carlo 57 (MCMC), are a classical approach for sampling from unnormalized target densities. The key idea is to 58 construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution matches the target distribution [10]. Prominent 59 examples include the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm [20, 29], Gibbs sampling [19], and Langevin 60 dynamics [35, 40]. By exploiting geometric structure in the target distribution, Hamiltonian Monte 61 Carlo [10, 12, 14, 27] often leads to more efficient exploration. To address scalability challenges in 62 high-dimensional or large-dataset scenarios, stochastic gradient MCMC variants [12, 52, 56, 57] have 63 been introduced. Although these MCMC methods reduce per-step computational costs or improve 64 mixing, they remain inherently iterative, requiring many transitions to yield high-quality samples. 65 **Diffusion-based Samplers.** An alternative viewpoint frames sampling as an optimal control task 66 [7, 37, 38, 55], where controlled stochastic differential equations transport an initial distribution 67 to the target via a Schrödinger bridge [42, 43]. This approach has recently motivated numerous 68 diffusion-based sampling methods [11, 13, 18, 36, 50]. Further improvements have been explored 69 through Hamiltonian dynamics [8], intermediate resampling strategies [11], and physics-informed 70 neural networks to evolve densities [49]. Recent methods such as CMCD [51] jointly optimize 71 forward and backward diffusion dynamics. Additionally, theoretical connections between GFlowNets 72 [4, 5] and diffusion-based sampling have been investigated [6, 54]. For an extensive review of relevant 73 metrics and baseline samplers, see [9]. Current diffusion methods partially amortize sampling costs 74 in training but still require iterative inference-time generation. In this work, we fully amortize 75 exploration during training, enabling efficient single-step sampling at inference. 76 **Consistent Generative Models.** Recent work in generative modeling has introduced the notion 77 of consistency. Consistency models [26, 45, 46] learn direct mapping from any intermediate state 78 to the terminal state. Progressive distillation [28, 41] incrementally distills a trained diffusion 79 model into a more efficient version that takes half as many steps. Shortcut models [16] apply this 80 distillation principle during training, enabling direct learning of efficient transitions without relying 81 on a pretrained teacher. We extend this line of work to the setting of sampling from unnormalized 82 densities, assuming only pointwise access to the target density ω, without requiring data samples.

## 151 **5 Self–Consistent Diffusion Samplers** 152 **5.1 Overivew**

153 Even though CDDS demonstrates that single-step sampling is feasible, it requires a pretrained 154 diffusion sampler for distillation. In this section, we further introduce *self-consistent diffusion* 155 *sampler* (SCDS) that achieves single-step sampling without requiring the pre-trained diffusion 156 sampler. The key idea is to adapt consistency training into the diffusion-based sampler training. 157 However, the challenge arises from merging two perspectives: diffusion-based samplers learn a 158 time-dependent control function that steers an SDE from a simple prior distribution to the target 159 distribution. Typically, the control is trained on a fixed schedule (e.g., N small increments of length 160 *T /N* along a discretized time axis), requiring multiple steps. In contrast, consistency models learn 161 a direct mapping from any intermediate state on an ODE to the terminal state. In other words, at 162 time t the model is implicitly taught to jump a large step of length T ↑ t. Crucially, consistency 163 training in the original formulation [45, 46] assumes the availability of intermediate states generated 164 by perturbing real data; in the context of sampling from unnormalized densities, however, we cannot 165 generate these states directly because we lack data and thus must learn both sampling and self166 distillation simultaneously. Furthermore, as discussed in Section 3, diffusion-based samplers typically 167 parameterize the control function, whose purpose differs fundamentally from that of the consistency 168 function used in standard consistency training. 169 To reconcile these perspectives and overcome this challenge, we propose conditioning the control 170 function uϑ(Xt*, t, d*) on both the current time t and the desired step size d. By adjusting d, the model 171 can adapt between short incremental steps (as in standard diffusion samplers) and large jumps (as in 172 consistency models). This design amortizes the learning of both small and large transitions into one 173 network and recovers consistency models' single-step sampling by setting d = T ↑ t and diffusion 174 sampling by setting d = *T /N*. In doing so, we avoid training with two contrasting learning targets, 175 hence making it feasible to train a single-step diffusion sampler from scratch.

## 176 **5.2 Training**

177 **Learning the base case** d = T/N. In standard generative modeling scenarios (where a dataset is 178 available), the base case d = *T /N* can be learned directly from data using deterministic trajectories 179 [25, 16]. These trajectories provide explicit guidance toward high-density regions of the target 180 distribution.

181 However, when working with an unnormalized density, a key challenge is discovering high-probability 182 regions [11]. Thus, the process (2) is particularly well-suited for learning the base case as the 183 Brownian motion helps probe different parts of the space, allowing the model to learn and adapt 184 itself to the target distribution. By optimizing uϑ(Xt, t, d = *T /N*) under (5), we ensure that the 185 model can generate meaningful transitions from the prior to these regions of interest, forming a strong 186 foundation for self-consistent learning at larger step sizes.

201 **End-to-end training algorithm.** As abovementioned, our training procedure jointly optimizes 202 two objectives: (i) a sampling loss (5) for the base case d = *T /N*, which ensures exploration and 203 score approximation by simulating the SDE (2), and (ii) the self-consistency loss (11) enforced on 204 the probability flow ODE of (2) for larger steps, which enforces consistency across multiple time 205 scales. This end-to-end formulation enables the model to fully amortize the cost of sampling into a 206 single forward pass at inference time. 207 To enable the recursive halving of steps, we discretize the time interval [0, T] into N + 1 points, 208 where N is chosen as a power of two. The sampling loss is computed by simulating the forward SDE 209 along this time grid.

210 For self-consistency training, we sample step sizes d and times t such that d are powers of two 211 (multiplied by *T /N*) dividing the remaining time T ↑ t. This ensures that from any time t, we can 212 take exactly k steps of size d to reach the terminal state for some integer k. This way, training focuses 213 on time sequences that are applicable during inference.

229 **Approximating the normalization constant.** Beyond sample generation, a common goal in 230 probabilistic inference is to estimate the normalization constant Z of the target density. Because 231 SCDS is formulated within the optimal control framework of stochastic sampling [7], it inherits a 232 natural estimator of Z through the Radon–Nikodym derivative between forward and backward path 233 measures. By discretizing the likelihood ratio in (6) along simulated trajectories, SCDS enables 234 efficient estimation of log Z. In contrast, consistency-based generative models [16, 46] are trained 235 using fully supervised losses on labeled data. As a result, they lack a built-in mechanism to estimate 236 Z or compute likelihood ratios.

## 237 **6 Experiments**

238 We empirically evaluate the sampling efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed CDDS and SCDS 239 across diverse standard benchmarks in Bayesian inference and sampling [9].

240 Specifically, we consider Bayesian posterior inference tasks, such as Ionosphere (35-D) and the 241 high-dimensional Log-Gaussian Cox Process (LGCP) (1600-D), alongside representative synthetic 242 targets: a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) of nine components in 2-D, a 2-D Many-Well with 32 243 well-separated modes (MW54), the widely-studied Funnel distribution in ten dimensions, and a 50-D 244 Many-Well distribution with 32 modes (MW52). Details are provided in Appendix We report the Sinkhorn distance W2ϖ 245 , the Effective Sample Size (ESS), and the absolute estimation 246 error of the log normalization constant ! log Z for tasks with accessible ground-truth samples. For 247 the real-world Ionosphere and LGCP tasks, we report the evidence lower bound (ELBO). ELBO, 248 ESS and !log Z rely on importance weights. With N > 1 we use the discretized RND of (6); for 249 N = 1 the running-cost term vanishes and the weight reduces to the boundary likelihood, which can potentially inflate ELBO scores. Hence we regard W2ϖ 250 and ! log Z as primary quality indicators, and 251 report ELBO only when ground truth samples are not available. 252 We benchmark our methods against established diffusion-based samplers: the Path Integral Sampler 253 (PIS) [55], the Denoising Diffusion Sampler (DDS) [50], both trained with the KL divergence, and 254 the Time-Reversed Diffusion Sampler (DIS) [7] trained with log-variance divergence. 255 Throughout our experiments, we use a pre-trained DIS as the teacher model for CDDS. Furthermore, 256 since the optimization problem (4) may admit infinitely many solutions, we fix the noising process (3) 257 in SCDS to ensure uniqueness of the learned solution, following the same approach as in DIS. 258 Detailed implementation and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix A.1. The code is available 259 at this repository.

## 297 **7 Conclusion**

298 We introduced two novel approaches for efficient sampling from unnormalized target distributions: 299 *consistency-distilled diffusion samplers* (CDDS) and the *self-consistent diffusion sampler* (SCDS). 300 CDDS uses consistency distillation without generating a large dataset of samples. SCDS requires no 301 pre-trained samplers and simultaneously learns to sample high-density regions and to take large steps 302 across the path space. Our empirical results across a range of benchmarks demonstrated that both 303 methods achieve competitive accuracy with as few as one or two steps. These findings highlighted 304 the potential of consistency-based methods for sampling from unnormalized densities.

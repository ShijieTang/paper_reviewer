## Abstract

1 Large-batch training sizes promise near-linear speedups in language model pertain2 ing, yet existing studies highlight its poor optimization dynamics and degraded final 3 performance. In this paper, we seek to understand the failure of large-batch train4 ing, and show that it can in fact substantially outperform conventional small-batch 5 training. We first identify a critical oversight in the conventional view: large-batch 6 training can substantially surpass small-batch baselines when provided sufficient 7 tokens, but this advantage is often unrecognized due to its initial poor optimization 8 dynamics, manifested as larger gradient norms and even worse per-step loss during 9 early warm-up phases. To address this, we introduce a simple batch size scheduler 10 that stabilizes and improves training at remarkably large batch sizes. Our scheduler 11 scales pretraining up to batches of 32M tokens, using 3.3× fewer computes to 12 achieve the superior later-stage performance of large-batch training. Detailed analy13 ses on gradient dynamics reveal that batch size fundamentally changes optimization 14 geometry. Notably, we show that classic gradient noise scale metrics fail to predict 15 the optimal batch size. Our findings offer practical recipes for designing efficient 16 and effective pretraining pipelines, and deepen the theoretical understanding of 17 large-batch optimization dynamics in language model pre-training.

## 18 **1 Introduction** 51 **2 Background And Experiment Setup**

52 Batch size is an important hyperparameter in deep learning, yet its optimal tuning remains unclear [24, 53 8]. Some early studies argue that large batch training can hinder model performance, particularly 54 in terms of generalization, suggesting that overly large batch sizes may be suboptimal. However, 55 recent studies propose that the critical batch size [20]—the maximum batch size that maintains 56 computational efficiency—scales with increasing data size. This implies that in contemporary large57 scale pretraining scenarios, larger batch sizes might be preferable. Despite this, determining the 58 optimal batch size remains an open question. 59 **Large batch training can hinder model generalization.** Using excessively large batch sizes 60 in training deep neural networks can negatively impact model generalization. Keskar et al.[15] 61 observed that large-batch training tends to converge to sharp minima, which generally exhibit poorer 62 generalization compared to the flatter minima associated with smaller batch sizes. Takase et al.[29] 63 explained this by noting that reduced gradient noise in large-batch training restricts the model's ability 64 to escape narrow minima. Similarly, Oyedotun et al. [22] argued that large batch sizes might lead to 65 near-rank deficiencies in activation tensors, thereby adversely affecting the optimization process and 66 generalization capability.

67 **Optimal batch size scales with the data size.** On the other hand, recent research has started to 68 explore batch scaling laws, examining how batch size relates to model size, data size, and compute.

43 Through detailed analyses of optimization metrics such as gradient norms, gradient noise, and 44 optimizer update direction, we offer explanations for why different batch size schedules ultimately 45 converge to similar final losses via a stabilization statement. We observe that classic gradient noise 46 scale [20] metrics fail to predict optimal batch sizes accurately, highlighting the need for new metrics 47 or insights into large-batch optimization dynamics. 48 Overall, our findings provide practical guidelines for designing more efficient pretraining strategies 49 and deepen our theoretical understanding of how large-batch training dynamics influence language 50 model pretraining. 19 Training language models with extremely large batch sizes unlocks near-linear speedups through data 20 parallelism, slashing wall-clock communication time on multi-node clusters - an advantage that 21 is especially pronounced for Mixture-of-Experts models [31, 5]. Yet, today's empirical evidence is 22 discouraging: large-batch training frequently results in worse optimization dynamics and substantially 23 degraded final model performance [15, 27, 20]. 24 In this paper, we seek to understand the purported failure of large-batch training, and show that 25 large-batch training is in fact viable. Specifically, we find that with a simple batch size scheduler, 26 large-batch size training can substantially surpass the performance of small-batch baselines.

27 We first re-examine the traditional "large batch hurts training" results (Figure 1 left) and reveal its 28 blind spot: when training on a sufficient amount of data, even naive constant large-batch size training 29 shows clear advantages (Figure 1 right). Recent research on batch size scaling laws also supports this 30 observation with the finding that optimal batch size scales primarily with data size [17, 32]. 31 Taking a closer look at the training dynamics, we find that large batch sizes indeed impede optimiza32 tion in the early training stage. It may be expected that large batch sizes will converge more slowly in 33 terms of token efficiency due to fewer total optimization steps. However, counterintuitively, we find 34 large-batch training is worse even in terms of optimization steps, despite consuming substantially 35 more tokens than the small-batch baseline. Further analysis shows that large-batch training exhibits 36 unstable gradient norms in the early stage. 37 To address this, we study a straightforward batch size scheduler: training with small batch sizes in the 38 beginning to exploit its superior early optimization dynamics, then gradually increasing to the target 39 large batch sizes to fully leverage its efficiency advantage. Specifically, we consistently improve 40 training with a batch size of 8M tokens from the early stages through to completion. For extremely 41 large batch sizes (e.g., 32M tokens), which initially fail within a given training budgets (e.g., 100B 42 tokens), our batch size scheduler successfully enables effective large batch training.

## 148 **3.3 Summary**

149 Based on our experiment results, we conclude that large-batch optimization fails at the early stage 150 of training, and warm-up is one of the important factors that causes significant impacts on the 151 performance of large-batch training. 152 However, both fixed-token and fixed-step warm-up schedules show inherent limitations. In short, it is 153 ill-advised to warm up with a large batch size.

## 154 **4 Batch Size Scheduler Unlocks Effective Large-Batch Training**

155 Large batch sizes significantly improve large-scale training efficiency and boost final performance 156 given sufficient data. Yet they often lag behind smaller batch sizes in the early training stage, and 157 it is hard to mitigate their inherent optimization problem simply via tweaking early-stage training 158 hyperparameters.

159 In this section, we show how a *batch size scheduler* delivers the best of both worlds: fast early 160 progress reminiscent of small batches and the strong asymptotic performance of large batches.

161 We firstly start with a preliminary example that replace only the warm-up phase with small batch size 162 and show its effectiveness. Then, we introduce a *linear batch size scheduler* can be competitive with 163 both small batch training in the early stage and large batch training in terms of final performance.

164 Moreover, batch size scheduler can effectively enable an extremely large batch training where the 165 straightforward constant batch size degrades. Finally, we give a possible explanation in the perspective 166 of hyperparameter and training process.

## 178 **4.2 Batch Size Scheduler**

179 Replacing warm-up phase with small BS has achieved a significant improvement. In this section, 180 we show that introducing a simple batch size scheduler strategy can further improves the large batch 181 training. A simple linear batch size scheduler make large batch both competitive with small batch in 182 the early and keep the advantage of large batch at convergence. 183 Similar to a learning rate scheduler, a batch size scheduler works by adjusting the batch size during 184 the training process. In this work, we focus on linear batch size schedulers. Formally, given an initial batch size Binit, a target batch size Btarget 185 , a start token count P, and a ramp length E, we lineary 186 interpolate the batch size from Binit at P tokens to Btarget at P + E tokens. Figure 5 (a) and (c) shows 187 all the batch size schedulers that we use in the experiments. As shown in Figure 5 (a), the scheduler 188 divides E tokens into equal-sized segments and uses a single batch size at each segment.

## 210 **4.4 A Possible Explanation**

203 A suddenly drastic change of BS can make the gradient a big change, which could possibly cause 204 instability problem in the training process. However, experiments show this aggressive strategy works 205 well. Due to the ease of implementation, the 0B ramp length has more potential into practical use. 206 In Sec. 4.4, we explain that the target batch size determines local optimization geometry, such as 207 gradient norms and noise levels, whereas the specific schedule shape has minimal impact. This 208 explains why even a schedule with sudden jump (0B ramp length) can perform well, motivating our 209 choice of a simple batch size scheduler. 211 In this section, we offer a possible explanation for why different batch size schedules ultimately 212 converge to similar final losses via a stabilization statement. Generally speaking, The stabilization 213 statement argues that the local optimization geometry is determined by current hyperparameters and 214 enough training time. 215 **The optimization geometry can adapt to the hyperparameters.** When we use fixed hyperparameters3 216 and train the model for a certain number of tokens, the local optimization geometry will adapt 217 to this hyperparameter stabilize. More specifically, we suspect there is a pre-stabilized stage and a 218 stabilized stage during the model training. In the stabilization stage, the gradient-related quantities - 219 including stochastic gradient noise, population gradient norm, first moment, second moment, and the 220 update direction in Adam - will stabilize to a value determined by hyperparameters. And finally, the 221 loss plateau in stabilization phase. 222 We provide evidence to our argument: In Figure 6 (a), we use the gradient noise tr(Σ) as an example 223 to show that in each hyperparameter configuration, tr(Σ) does stabilize, and the stabilized value 224 depends on hyperparameters (BS in this case). 225 **The stabilization is universal to training history.** We observe that this stabilization property does 226 not depend significantly on the training history. Specifically, regardless of previous hyperparameter 227 choices or scheduling strategies, the stabilized optimization geometry becomes consistent after 228 training for enough tokens using the same final hyperparameters. 229 Empirically, we confirm this through two experiments comparing optimization metrics across batch 230 size schedulers with the same final batch size. Figure 6 (b) shows the gradient noise tr(Σ). The 231 constant batch size baseline maintains a stable gradient noise from 15B to 100B tokens. In contrast, 232 schedulers initially exhibit increasing noise as the batch size ramps up, eventually stabilizing at the 233 baseline level. Figure 6 (c) shows the norm of Adam update direction ∥u∥. Again, all schedulers 234 eventually stabilize at the baseline value. However, the scheduler shape affects stabilization speed: a 235 slower increase in batch size results in smoother stabilization. 236 **The length of pre-stabilization stage varies.** Now, we focus on the pre-stabilization stage. The 237 length of this stage can vary significantly between different batch sizes. We observe that introducing 238 a large batch size too early prolongs this phase.

## 249 **5 Discussion**

252 **Gradient noise scale cannot predict critical batch size.** McCandlish et al. [20] introduces the 253 gradient noise scale (GNS), defined in Section 4.4, and argue that it can be used to predict the critical 254 batch size (CBS). They also mentioned that GNS depends on the learning rate via a "temperature" 255 mechanism, and claimed that GNS prescribes an optimal batch size at any given temperature. To 256 verify this point, we use a linear warm-up and constant learning rate (LR), use various batch size (BS) 257 ranging from 0.5M to 32M, train on 300B tokens, and compute the GNS for intermediate checkpoints. 258 In Figure 7 (a), we firstly see that GNS gradually stabilizes with some variance, which verifies our 259 stabilization statement in Section 4.4 again. Surprisingly, we find the when using a BS greater than or 260 equal to 2M, the stabilized value is far less than the BS used, indicating that predicted CBS is around 261 0.3M-0.5M and a BS larger than 2M should exhibit a significant performance degradation. However, 262 the large BS will eventually surpass the small BS in performance as tokens processed increase even 263 in the per-token axis (recall Figure 1). Additionally, the stabilized GNS decreases as the BS increase. 264 This means the GNS cannot predict CBS in a straightforward manner and the relationship between 265 GNS and CBS needs rethinking.

266 **The implicit bias of Adam: update direction primarily adapts to batch size.** As we show in 267 Section 4.4, when we train the model with fixed LR and BS for at least a certain amount of data, the 268 gradient-related metric will adapt to this LR and BS, and stabilize. We further explore the functional 269 relationship of the stabilized value to LR and BS. In Figure 7 (b)(c), we find the stabilized norm of 270 Adam update direction ∥u∥, i.e., magnitude of update before multiplying the LR, primarily depends 271 on BS and is almost independent of LR. This is unexpected since the stabilized gradient, both in terms of signal (∥g∥
2 272 ) and noise (tr(Σ)), does depend on LR at a fixed BS, while the gradient induced 273 Adam update are not. We attribute this phenomenon to a new form BS-related *implicit bias of Adam*.

239 Figure 6 (d) shows that the gradient norm stabilizes faster for smaller batch sizes than for larger 240 ones, especially at a batch size of 32M. This indicates the poor early-stage performance with large 241 batches may result from slower stabilization, and using smaller batch sizes initially can accelerate 242 stabilization.

243 **Summary.** In this section, we propose a stabilization perspective to explain training dynamics and 244 optimization geometry under batch size scheduling. In the pre-stabilized stage, small batch size helps 245 to stabilize faster, and make the early stage performance better. After transitioning to a large batch 246 size and training further, the model adapts and benefits from the improved optimization efficiency of 247 large-batch training. Additionally, our stabilization perspective implies that the exact speed of batch 248 size scheduler is less important over a sufficiently long training period. 250 In this section, we discuss more findings and insights from our experiments beyond the implications 251 on batch size scheduler strategy. 274 Understanding this phenomenon will probably help us figure out the algorithmic impact on optimal 275 BS of different optimizers.

## 276 **6 Related Work**

277 **Batch size ramp-up.** Early literature propose to increase the batch size during the training process 278 in order to reduce the number of parameter updates [4] or replace the learning rate decay [27], to 279 improve training efficiency. In these works, batch size ramping up to 524288 images per batch [4] 280 can achieve similar accuracies to small batch sizes. However, they operate in a relatively outdated 281 experiment setting. The most significant difference in settings is that they do multi-epoch training 282 while currently people use one-pass training particularly in the language models pretraining. 283 Recently, many technical reports on language model pretraining utilize the batch size ramp-up 284 technique. Specifically, Llama 3 [9] increases batch size from a initial 4M tokens to 8M at 252M 285 tokens, and then to 16M at 2.87T tokens. DeepSeek-V3 [3] gradually increases batch size from 286 12.6M to 63M in the training of the first 469B tokens, and keeps 63M afterwards. MiniMax-01 [21]
287 fits a batch size scaling law w.r.t. loss and doubles the batch size whenever loss reaches this fitted 288 line. In the specific pre-training process, they increases batch size from a initial 16M to 32M at 69B 289 tokens, then to 64M at 790B tokens, and finally to 128M at 4.7T tokens. MiniCPM [13] doubles 290 the batch size from 2M to 4M at about 500B tokens. Compared to the original batch ramp-up, these 291 works tend to do batch size ramp-up mainly in the *early stage* of training, and up to a *mild* global 292 batch size. However, They do not offer a principled guideline about using this technique.

293 **Hyperparameter scaling laws.** Beyond the scaling laws of loss w.r.t. model size and data 294 amount [14], and compute-optimal scaling laws [12], researchers start to study hyperparameter 295 scaling laws [1, 2, 13, 17, 23, 26, 30]: the relationship between optimal hyperparameters - typi296 cally learning rate and batch size - and interested independent metric, including model size, data 297 amount, compute, or even loss value. Serval works study the batch size scaling with expected loss 298 value [14, 13, 30, 26], thus they cannot predict the optimal batch size a priori. Other work study 299 optimal batch size as a function of model, data, or compute [1, 2, 17, 23]. Their results are typically 300 like optimal learning rate becomes smaller and optimal batch size becomes larger when the compute 301 budget increases. 302 **Critical batch size.** It was argued that there exists a critical batch size (CBS): increasing the batch 303 size up to the CBS results in minimal degradation, while further increasing it beyond the CBS yields 304 unneglectable performance degradation. Previous studies suggest that when the batch size is lower 305 than this critical value, the learning rate needs to be proportionally adjusted according to batch 306 size [8, 11]. McCandlish et al. [20] introduces the gradient noise scale, and argue that it can be used 307 to predict the CBS. Follow-up works talk about the efficient computation for gradient noise scale [10] 308 and the extension to Adam [18].

## 309 **7 Limitations And Conclusion**

310 **Limitations.** One limitation of our work is that we do not verify all of our findings on larger-scale 311 language models such as 1B or 8B, due to our limited computational resources. However, we observe 312 similar phenomenon based on our preliminary experiment results on 1B models, such as 1) poor 313 early-stage optimization of large-batch training and 2) effectiveness of batch size scheduler. In 314 addition, as shown in prior work about critical batch size, the optimal batch size is often independent 315 of model parameters. We leave more analysis about our findings on larger models to future work. 316 Another limitation of work is that we only focus on linear batch size schedule. Future work can study 317 more sophisticated batch size scheduler following prior work on learning rate scheduler. 318 **Conclusion.** Enabling extremely large batch size pre-training is a fundamental problem for efficient 319 modern language model pre-training. Existing empirical evidence highlights the poor optimization 320 dynamics and the degraded final performance of large-batch training. However, through detailed 321 analysis of the optimization dynamics of large-batch training over a long horizon, we show that 1) 322 large-batch training mainly suffers from poor its early-stage optimization, but has superior perfor323 mance in the later stage. The empirical success of our batch size scheduler, alongside our theoretical 324 understanding of large-batch optimization dynamics, suggests that it is promising to enable more 325 efficient and effective language model pre-training at extremely large batch size scales.
